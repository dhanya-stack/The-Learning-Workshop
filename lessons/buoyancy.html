Buoyancy and Floating Objects

How can an enormous steel ship float when a small steel nail sinks?

Why some objects float

A small steel nail sinks, yet a large steel ship can float. The difference is not that water “supports heavy things.” It is how much water the whole object can displace before it becomes fully submerged.

Think of lowering a block into water. Water pressure pushes on every wetted surface. Because pressure increases with depth, the upward force on the bottom is generally greater than the downward force on the top. The overall vertical difference is the buoyant force.

A floating object settles at a depth where this upward buoyant force exactly balances its weight. That might be a shallow immersion for cork or a much deeper immersion for a densely loaded boat. An object can be made of metal and still have a low average density if it encloses enough air.

Density is useful—but volume matters too

For a freely floating object in a liquid, the density of the complete object, including hollow spaces, must be lower than the liquid's density. If it is the same, the object can be neutrally buoyant; if higher, it normally sinks unless another force supports it. Changing the shape of a fixed mass does not change its material density, but a hollow shape changes the volume and average density of the entire structure.

Archimedes’ principle

Imagine temporarily replacing a submerged object's shape with the exact volume of water occupying that space. Before the object arrived, the surrounding water supported this volume of water in equilibrium. The net upward force from the surrounding pressure must therefore equal the weight of that displaced water. This leads to Archimedes' principle:

Fb = ρfluid g Vdisplaced

Here ρ is the surrounding fluid density, g is gravitational acceleration and V is the submerged volume displacing fluid—not always the total volume of the object.

For a free object floating at rest, Fb equals its weight mg. Hence ρfluidgVsubmerged = mg. For a uniform object floating in a uniform liquid, that rearranges to:

Submerged fraction = ρobject, average / ρfluid

For instance, if an object's average density is 600 kg/m³ and the water density is 1,000 kg/m³, roughly 60% of its total volume must sit below the surface at equilibrium. If the ratio exceeds one, no partially submerged equilibrium is possible.

Why depth alone does not increase buoyancy

Pressure grows with depth, but the pressure on the top and bottom of a rigid fully submerged object both grow as the object moves deeper. In an incompressible fluid of constant density, the net difference—and therefore buoyant force—stays essentially unchanged when its submerged volume stays constant. A compressible object, or one in a fluid whose density varies, can behave differently.

Worked examples

Example 1 — Displacement. A floating object weighs 19.62 N. How much freshwater does it displace? Use ρ = 1,000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s².

Step 1. Floating equilibrium means Fb = W = 19.62 N.

Step 2. Rearrange Fb = ρgV to V = W/(ρg).

Step 3. V = 19.62/(1000×9.81) = 0.002 m³ = 2 litres.

Notice that the required displacement depends on weight, not directly on whether the object is made of wood, plastic or steel.

Example 2 — How far underwater? A uniform rectangular block has density 750 kg/m³ and floats in freshwater of density 1,000 kg/m³. What fraction of its height is submerged?

Step 1. At rest, ρwatergVsubmerged = ρblockgVtotal.

Step 2. Divide both sides by ρwatergVtotal: fraction = 750/1000 = 0.75.

Result: about 75% of its height is below the surface, provided its cross-section is uniform.

Example 3 — Weight versus buoyancy. A completely submerged sealed object displaces 0.004 m³ of freshwater and weighs 50 N. Does it rise or sink at first, neglecting drag?

Step 1. Buoyancy is 1000 × 9.81 × 0.004 = 39.24 N upward.

Step 2. Net force is 39.24 − 50 = −10.76 N (taking upward positive).

Conclusion: it initially accelerates downward. Fluid drag will influence its subsequent motion.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. What buoyant force acts on an object displacing 0.001 m³ of freshwater?

Show worked answer

F_b = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.001 = 9.81 N.

Problem 2 of 5

Displace 1 L water; g=10 m/s². Buoyant force?

Show answer and method

1000×10×0.001=10 N.

Problem 3 of 5

For floating equilibrium, what equals the object weight?

Show answer and method

Buoyant force.

Problem 4 of 5

Does an object sink when its average density exceeds that of the fluid?

Show answer and method

Normally yes, if no other supporting forces.

Problem 5 of 5

Does buoyancy require an object to be fully submerged?

Show answer and method

No; partially submerged floating objects also experience buoyancy.

Try it yourself

Use a bowl of water and a small piece of modelling clay. First roll it into a solid ball; then reshape the same clay into an open boat with a wide base. Can the boat displace enough water to support its weight while the ball cannot?

For a second experiment, float an empty plastic container and add coins one at a time. Observe the waterline. Each extra coin increases weight, so the container has to displace more water to restore equilibrium. Stop before it overflows, and keep the experiment away from electronic devices.

When the simple picture changes

Archimedes' principle still applies to submerged objects, but the density-ratio rule describes a simple floating equilibrium, not every situation. Surface tension matters at very small scales (for example, some insects), while tethering, trapped gas, changing fluid density, and compressible materials can modify what happens. For a moving object, drag and acceleration must also be considered.
