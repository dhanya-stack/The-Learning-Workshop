Introduction to Differential Equations

What happens when a system’s rate of change depends on its current state?

An equation for changing quantities

A differential equation describes a rule for change rather than giving a direct formula for the state. Such equations model cooling, population growth, spring motion and electric circuits. An initial condition selects one particular solution.

Some scientific laws describe not the quantity itself but how quickly it changes. Newton’s second law relates acceleration to force; a cooling law may relate temperature-change rate to the temperature difference from the surroundings. A differential equation captures that relationship and asks what function of time satisfies it.

This is why differential equations connect disparate subjects. The same mathematical pattern can describe water draining from a tank, radioactive decay, and the voltage across a discharging capacitor, though the physical meanings and parameters differ. The equation tells us how a system evolves; its initial condition tells us which particular evolution we are observing.

Relating a function to its rate of change

In a simple growth model, rate is proportional to amount: dy/dt = ky. The solution has the form y(t) = y₀e^(kt), where k controls growth or decay. A positive k produces growth, a negative k decay.

dy/dt = ky; y(t) = y₀e^(kt)

Consider dy/dt=−ky with constant k>0. It says that the rate of decrease is proportional to the amount remaining. A solution is y(t)=Ce^(−kt), because differentiating it gives −k times itself. If y(0)=y₀, then C=y₀. The time constant 1/k gives a useful sense of how quickly the process changes.

Not every differential equation has a simple closed-form solution, and a mathematically valid equation may be a poor physical model outside its assumptions. Real cooling coefficients can depend on airflow and temperature; real drag may not be proportional to speed. Later, numerical stepping methods help compute approximate trajectories when analytical solutions are difficult.

Worked examples

Question. A quantity starts at 100 units and obeys dy/dt = 0.1y per hour. Estimate its amount after 3 hours.

Solution, step by step. With y₀ = 100 and k = 0.1 h⁻¹, y(3) = 100e^0.3 ≈ 134.99 units. Its rate increases as the amount increases.

Worked example 2

Question. A hot object cools with dT/dt = −0.2(T−20), with T(0)=80°C. Find T(5).

Solution. Separate variables: T−20 = Ce^(−0.2t). Initially C=60. Thus T(5)=20+60e^(−1)≈42.1°C.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. If y₀ = 80 and k = −0.2 h⁻¹, approximately how much remains after 5 hours?

Show worked answer

y(5) = 80e⁻¹ ≈ 29.43 units.

Problem 2 of 5

Does y=e²ˣ solve dy/dx=2y?

Show answer and method

Yes: y′=2e²ˣ=2y.

Problem 3 of 5

Solve dy/dx=3 with y(0)=2.

Show answer and method

y=3x+2.

Problem 4 of 5

What extra information selects one solution from y′=2x?

Show answer and method

An initial or boundary condition; general solution y=x²+C.

Problem 5 of 5

If y′=−ky with k>0 and y>0, is y rising or falling?

Show answer and method

Falling: its derivative is negative.

Try it yourself

Start with 100 and repeatedly add 10% of the current value in a spreadsheet. Compare discrete updates with the continuous exponential model.

Where this model stops

The exponential rule assumes k stays constant and no capacity limit intervenes; real systems often violate these assumptions.
