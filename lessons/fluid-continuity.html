Conservation of Mass and Fluid Flow

Why does water speed up when a pipe becomes narrower?

Where does the moving fluid go?

Mass cannot disappear between two sections of a closed pipe. In steady flow with no leaks, mass entering each second equals mass leaving. If fluid density stays approximately constant, a smaller flow area requires higher mean velocity.

Imagine squeezing the nozzle on a garden hose. Water often leaves the smaller opening faster, even though the hose is supplied at roughly the same overall flow rate. Water is not disappearing inside the nozzle; the same amount of mass must cross successive sections each second under steady conditions without leaks.

The distinction between volume flow and mass flow is essential. For an incompressible liquid, conserving mass usually also conserves volume flow. For a compressible gas, density can change substantially, so equal mass flow can correspond to different volume flow rates along a duct.

Conservation of mass

Mass flow rate is density × cross-sectional area × mean speed. For incompressible steady flow through two sections, A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. This statement is about average section velocities, not necessarily each point in the flow.

ṁ = ρAv; A₁v₁ = A₂v₂

Mass flow rate is ṁ=ρAv for uniform average velocity normal to a cross-section. For steady flow in a single passage with no additions or removals, ρ₁A₁v₁=ρ₂A₂v₂. If density is effectively constant, the factors ρ cancel, giving A₁v₁=A₂v₂.

Suppose a water pipe narrows from 40 mm to 20 mm diameter. Its cross-sectional area becomes one quarter as large, so average speed must become four times greater if flow is steady and incompressible. This does not mean pressure automatically rises or falls by a particular amount; momentum and energy equations and frictional losses are needed for those questions. Always use actual inner diameters for pipe calculations.

Worked examples

Question. Water flows at 2 m/s through a pipe area of 0.004 m², then enters a narrower region with area 0.001 m². Find speed.

Solution, step by step. Volume flow = 0.004 × 2 = 0.008 m³/s. New speed = 0.008/0.001 = 8 m/s.

Worked example 2

Question. In steady incompressible flow, area changes from 0.04 to 0.01 m²; inlet speed is 2 m/s. Find exit speed.

Solution. Conservation of volume flow: A₁v₁=A₂v₂. v₂=0.04×2/0.01=8 m/s.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A pipe narrows from area 6 cm² to 3 cm². If speed before narrowing is 1 m/s, find speed after.

Show worked answer

A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ gives v₂ = (6/3) × 1 = 2 m/s.

Problem 2 of 5

If A=0.02 m² and v=3 m/s, find volume flow rate.

Show answer and method

Q=Av=0.06 m³/s.

Problem 3 of 5

Why does fluid speed rise through a narrowing pipe in steady incompressible flow?

Show answer and method

The same volume must cross every section per unit time.

Problem 4 of 5

For a compressible gas, which flow rate is conserved in steady flow?

Show answer and method

Mass flow rate, ρAv.

Problem 5 of 5

Convert 0.001 m³/s to L/s.

Show answer and method

1 L/s.

Try it yourself

Compare water leaving a tap when the opening is partly narrowed. Sketch a control volume spanning two cross-sections.

Where this model stops

Compressible gas flow requires accounting for density changes; the simple Av = constant rule may not apply.
