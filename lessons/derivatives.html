Derivatives: understanding change

From a car’s speed to a cooling object: learn to measure how fast something changes at a single instant.

What does a derivative mean?

A car's trip meter might tell you how far it has travelled, but it doesn't directly tell you how fast it is moving right now. A derivative answers questions about the instantaneous rate of change.

On a distance–time graph, the slope between two points describes average speed over an interval. The slope at a single point describes instantaneous speed.

It helps to distinguish a measurement from how that measurement changes. A thermometer tells you the temperature at one instant; repeated readings tell you whether the room is warming quickly or slowly. The derivative describes the local rate of change, even when that rate varies from moment to moment. On a graph it is the slope of the tangent line, but in an experiment it might represent speed, heating rate or flow sensitivity.

A positive derivative means a quantity is increasing as the input increases, and a negative derivative means it is decreasing. Zero slope does not necessarily mean nothing interesting is happening: an object thrown upward has zero vertical velocity at the very top, although gravity is still accelerating it downward. To interpret any derivative, always name the quantity being differentiated and its units.

From an average to an instant

Consider the simple function s(t) = t². In an imaginary motion model, s is distance in metres and t is time in seconds.

Average speed = [s(t + h) − s(t)] / h

For t = 2 and h = 1, the average speed is (9 − 4) / 1 = 5 m/s. But if we make the time interval very small, the average speed approaches 4 m/s.

Derivative = lim (h → 0) [s(t + h) − s(t)] / h

For s(t) = t², expanding (t + h)² gives [2th + h²] / h = 2t + h. As h approaches zero, the derivative becomes 2t.

The formal idea begins with an average rate over an interval: change in output divided by change in input. Shrink that interval while watching whether the average approaches one stable value. If it does, that limit is the derivative at the point. The process is not merely an algebraic trick; it explains why the instantaneous rate is meaningful.

For s(t)=t², the average rate from t to t+h is ((t+h)²-t²)/h = 2t+h when h≠0. As h approaches zero, the rate approaches 2t. If s is in metres and t is in seconds, this derivative has units m/s. If the graph has a sharp corner, the slope from the left and the slope from the right may disagree, so a derivative need not exist there. Later, derivatives will let us optimise designs and model acceleration.

Worked examples

Problem: A toy car's position follows s(t) = 2t² + 3t metres. What is its instantaneous velocity at t = 4 seconds?
  1. Differentiate: ds/dt = 4t + 3.
  2. Put t = 4: velocity = 4(4) + 3 = 19 m/s.
  3. Interpretation: at that instant, its position is increasing at 19 metres per second in the chosen positive direction.

This model is a mathematical example, not a typical real toy-car speed.

Worked example 2

Question. The position of a particle is x(t) = t³ − 3t metres. Find its velocity at t = 2 s.

Solution. Differentiate: v(t) = 3t² − 3. Substitute t = 2: v(2) = 12 − 3 = 9 m/s.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5
Your turn: The temperature of an object is modelled by T(t) = 20 + 6t − t² °C. At t = 2 minutes, is it heating or cooling, and how quickly?
Show worked answer

dT/dt = 6 − 2t. At t = 2, dT/dt = +2 °C/min, so it is heating at that instant.

Problem 2 of 5

Differentiate f(x) = 7x² − 4x + 2.

Show answer and method

f′(x) = 14x − 4.

Problem 3 of 5

If s(t) = 5t² metres, what is the velocity at 3 s?

Show answer and method

v = 10t, so 30 m/s.

Problem 4 of 5

Where is f(x) = x² − 6x stationary?

Show answer and method

f′ = 2x−6 = 0 at x = 3.

Problem 5 of 5

What does a negative derivative tell you about a graph?

Show answer and method

The function is decreasing at that point, provided the derivative exists.

Try it yourself

Walk at a steady pace while someone records your position every second. Plot distance against time. A nearly straight line has an almost constant slope. Try speeding up: what happens to the slope?
