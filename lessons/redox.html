Oxidation, Reduction and Electrochemistry

Why does a battery work, and how is rust related to electron transfer?

Following electrons through a reaction

Oxidation involves loss of electrons; reduction involves gain. These processes occur together because electrons cannot simply disappear. Separating the half-reactions into different parts of a cell can let electrons travel through a circuit.

Oxidation and reduction describe transfer of electrons or changes in formal oxidation state. When iron rusts, iron is oxidised while another species is reduced. In a battery, the separation of oxidation and reduction processes allows electron flow through an external circuit and provides electrical energy.

The mnemonic “oxidation is loss, reduction is gain” refers to electrons. The two processes must occur together: electrons cannot disappear. A reaction may involve electron-density shifts in covalent species even when discrete free electrons are not actually moving between isolated ions.

Oxidation, reduction and electrical work

Oxidation numbers help track electron transfers. In a simple galvanic cell, electrons leave the oxidation electrode (anode) and travel through the external circuit to the reduction electrode (cathode). Ions move through an electrolyte to maintain charge balance.

Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ (oxidation)

Assign oxidation numbers according to conventions, then check which elements increase or decrease. In a simple zinc–copper galvanic cell, zinc is oxidised to Zn²⁺ at the anode while Cu²⁺ is reduced to copper at the cathode. Electrons pass through an external conductor, while ions in the electrolyte help maintain charge balance.

Cell voltage relates electrical driving force to reaction free energy through ΔG=−nFE under appropriate conditions. Standard electrode potentials help predict ideal-cell behaviour, but measured performance depends on concentrations, internal resistance, temperature and reaction kinetics. Household battery disassembly is not a safe experiment; paper-based half-reaction balancing is a better starting activity.

Worked examples

Question. When zinc metal forms Zn²⁺ ions, how many electrons are released for every zinc atom?

Solution, step by step. Zinc loses two electrons: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. The metal is oxidised; another half-reaction must consume those electrons.

Worked example 2

Question. Magnesium metal forms Mg²⁺. Write its oxidation half-equation and identify electron transfer.

Solution. Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻. Magnesium loses two electrons, so it is oxidised.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. In Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu, is copper oxidised or reduced?

Show worked answer

Reduced: Cu²⁺ gains two electrons to form neutral copper.

Problem 2 of 5

Does oxidation involve loss or gain of electrons?

Show answer and method

Loss.

Problem 3 of 5

In Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻, is zinc oxidised or reduced?

Show answer and method

Oxidised: zinc loses two electrons.

Problem 4 of 5

What is the oxidation state of elemental oxygen O₂?

Show answer and method

0.

Problem 5 of 5

What is the sign of the cathode in a standard galvanic cell?

Show answer and method

Positive; reduction occurs there.

Try it yourself

Sketch a two-electrode cell with a salt bridge and show separate paths for electrons and ions. Use a safe classroom kit for any real assembly.

Where this model stops

Cathode means reduction and anode means oxidation in both battery and electrolysis; electrode signs change with cell type.
