Vectors: magnitude and direction

How do you describe something that has both a size and a direction? Start with a walk, then discover how vectors combine.

What is a vector?

Imagine telling someone to walk five metres. That is a distance, but it does not say where to go. Now say: walk five metres east. You have given both a size and a direction. That is the idea of a vector.

Speed is a scalar: it has a size. Velocity is a vector: it has a size and a direction. The force exerted by a rocket engine is also a vector.

A number such as 20 can tell us a temperature or a mass, but it cannot by itself describe a journey. If two people each walk five metres, one east and one west, their distances match while their displacements differ. That is why a vector has both magnitude and direction. An arrow is a convenient representation, but the vector is the physical or mathematical quantity—not the ink on the page.

Consider directions while giving instructions to a robot. “Move three metres” is incomplete; “move three metres north” is usable. Later, when the robot must avoid an obstacle, we can combine several short movements into one resultant displacement. Think about the surprising consequence: it is possible to travel a considerable distance and have a zero resultant displacement when you return to your starting point.

Breaking a vector into components

On a flat surface, we can describe motion using two perpendicular directions: horizontal (x) and vertical (y). A vector of 3 m east and 4 m north has components (3, 4).

Magnitude = √(x² + y²)

Its magnitude is √(3² + 4²) = 5 m. This follows from a right triangle; no complicated new rule is required.

Vector addition describes the cumulative effect of directed quantities. Draw the second arrow starting at the first arrow's head; the arrow from the first tail to the final head represents the sum. Moving the arrows without rotating or scaling them does not change the answer, so the same calculation works whether you construct a triangle or a parallelogram.

Unit vectors help when a picture is no longer enough. In a plane, write a displacement as x units in the horizontal direction plus y units vertically. For a displacement (3, 4), the magnitude is sqrt(3²+4²)=5, while its direction relative to the positive x-axis is arctan(4/3). This is also why two forces of 10 N do not necessarily combine to 20 N: their directions matter. The same methods apply to velocities, accelerations and electric fields.

Explore: adding vectors

Two movements can be combined. Draw the second arrow from the tip of the first; the arrow from the original start to the final end is the resultant.

Worked examples

Problem: A walker travels 3 km east, then 4 km north. How far are they from their starting point? In what direction?
  1. Take east as x and north as y: (3, 4) km.
  2. Magnitude: √(3² + 4²) = 5 km.
  3. Direction: tan θ = 4/3, so θ ≈ 53.1° north of east.

The walker has walked 7 km along the path but is only 5 km from the start. Distance travelled and displacement are different.

Worked example 2

Question. A robot moves 6 m east and 8 m north. Find its displacement magnitude and direction.

Solution. The components are (6, 8) m. Magnitude = √(6²+8²) = 10 m. Direction = arctan(8/6) ≈ 53.1° north of east.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5
Your turn: A drone flies 2 m east and then 2 m south. What is its displacement?
Show worked answer

Components (2, −2) m. Magnitude = √(2² + (−2)²) = 2√2 ≈ 2.83 m; direction is 45° south of east.

Problem 2 of 5

What is the magnitude of the vector (5, 12) m?

Show answer and method

√(25+144) = 13 m.

Problem 3 of 5

Add the vectors (2, −1) and (−3, 4).

Show answer and method

Add components: (−1, 3).

Problem 4 of 5

What displacement results from 7 m east and 7 m west?

Show answer and method

Zero: the two displacements cancel.

Problem 5 of 5

A vector has magnitude 10 N and points due west. What are its east-positive x and north-positive y components?

Show answer and method

(−10, 0) N.

Try it in real life

Draw a map on graph paper. Let one square represent one metre. Trace two journeys using arrows. Now draw a single arrow connecting your first and last position. You have just constructed vector addition.
