pH, Indicators and Buffers

How can a solution resist big changes in pH after a little acid is added?

Why some solutions resist pH changes

pH expresses hydrogen-ion activity on a logarithmic scale. Buffers contain a weak acid/base pair that can consume modest added acid or base. Their effect has limits: once one partner is depleted, pH can change sharply.

A buffer is a solution that resists large changes in pH when relatively small amounts of acid or base are added. It usually contains a weak acid and its conjugate base, or a weak base and its conjugate acid. Each component can consume some of the added reagent, reducing the immediate shift in hydrogen-ion activity.

Buffers matter in biological systems, chemical analysis and industrial processing. But resistance is finite: a sufficiently large addition will overwhelm the available acid or base component. A buffer does not mean the pH can never change.

Acid–base balance and buffering

At introductory level pH ≈ −log₁₀[H⁺] when concentration is expressed relative to the standard reference. Henderson–Hasselbalch estimates buffer pH from pKa and the base-to-acid ratio for suitable dilute systems.

pH ≈ pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA])

For a simple weak-acid buffer under suitable activity approximations, the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation is pH≈pKa+log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]). When the acid and conjugate base concentrations are equal, pH is approximately pKa. Raising their ratio by a factor of ten increases predicted pH by about one unit, assuming the approximation remains valid.

A buffer works best near the acid's pKa and when there is enough total buffer concentration to handle additions. Strong dilution changes capacity even when the concentration ratio and initial pH change relatively little. Complex biological or concentrated solutions require more careful activity and equilibrium calculations.

Worked examples

Question. A buffer has pKa = 4.76 and equal concentrations of acid and conjugate base. Estimate pH.

Solution, step by step. Ratio = 1, so log₁₀(1) = 0. Therefore pH ≈ pKa = 4.76.

Worked example 2

Question. A buffer contains equal concentrations of weak acid HA and conjugate base A⁻. If pKa=4.8, estimate pH.

Solution. Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH=pKa+log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]) = 4.8 + log₁₀(1) = 4.8.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. If [A⁻]/[HA] = 10 with pKa = 4.76, estimate pH.

Show worked answer

pH ≈ 4.76 + log₁₀(10) = 5.76.

Problem 2 of 5

When [A⁻]/[HA]=10, what is pH relative to pKa?

Show answer and method

pH=pKa+1.

Problem 3 of 5

At pH 7, what is [H⁺] approximately in dilute aqueous solution?

Show answer and method

10⁻⁷ mol/L, as a concentration approximation.

Problem 4 of 5

What happens when a little strong acid is added to an effective buffer?

Show answer and method

Conjugate base consumes much of the added H⁺, limiting the pH change.

Problem 5 of 5

Can a buffer resist arbitrarily large acid addition?

Show answer and method

No; it has finite capacity.

Try it yourself

Compare pH changes in plain water and a safe prepared buffer with indicator strips, using small amounts and lab-safe guidance.

Where this model stops

pH is formally defined using activity; Henderson–Hasselbalch is an approximation and does not guarantee unlimited buffer capacity.
