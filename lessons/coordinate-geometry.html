Coordinate Geometry in 2D and 3D

How can we describe shapes and distances precisely in two or three dimensions?

Describing position with coordinates

Coordinates locate points relative to chosen axes. Once a point has numbers, you can calculate distances, directions and intersections without depending on a scale drawing. Three-dimensional coordinates extend the same idea with a z-axis.

Coordinates let us replace a sketch with precise statements about position. A point such as (3,4) means something only after we specify an origin, directions and units. That reference choice matters: a robot's coordinates relative to its own body differ from its coordinates relative to a room.

The same geometric object can be described in several systems. Cartesian coordinates are convenient for rectangular layouts, while polar coordinates are useful when distance and direction from a central point are more natural. Switching representations does not move the object; it changes how we describe it.

Distance, slope and direction

In 2D the distance between points follows from Pythagoras. In 3D, add the squared difference in the third direction. Midpoints use the average coordinate along each axis.

d = √((x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)² + (z₂−z₁)²)

In a plane, the distance between (x₁,y₁) and (x₂,y₂) follows from a right triangle: d=sqrt((x₂−x₁)²+(y₂−y₁)²). The slope of a nonvertical line is (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁); a vertical line needs a different description because its horizontal difference is zero.

For navigation, a point can be represented by radius r and angle θ, with x=r cosθ and y=r sinθ. In three dimensions, add z and use the same distance principle. Avoid treating coordinate numbers as physical truth without considering measurement uncertainty, axis conventions and whether your reference frame is rotating or translating.

Worked examples

Question. Find the distance between A(1,2,3) and B(4,6,3).

Solution, step by step. Coordinate differences are (3,4,0). Distance = √(3² + 4² + 0²) = 5 length units. The z-values match, so the separation lies in a plane.

Worked example 2

Question. Find the straight-line distance between A(1,2,3) and B(4,6,3).

Solution. Difference = (3,4,0); distance = √(3²+4²+0²)=5.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. Find the midpoint between (−2,4) and (6,8).

Show worked answer

Average x and y separately: ((−2+6)/2,(4+8)/2) = (2,6).

Problem 2 of 5

Find midpoint of (2,4) and (6,0).

Show answer and method

(4,2).

Problem 3 of 5

What is the gradient between (0,1) and (2,5)?

Show answer and method

(5−1)/(2−0)=2.

Problem 4 of 5

Find the distance from origin to (5,12).

Show answer and method

13.

Problem 5 of 5

Write the equation of a line through (0,3) with slope −2.

Show answer and method

y=−2x+3.

Try it yourself

Mark points for holes on a cardboard plate using two edges as reference axes. Use coordinates to plan a rectangular hole pattern.

Where this model stops

Coordinate differences must use consistent units and the same reference frame.
