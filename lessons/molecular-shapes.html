Molecular Shapes and Polarity

Why is water bent while carbon dioxide is linear?

Why molecules take particular shapes

Molecules have three-dimensional shapes because electron regions around an atom repel and arrange themselves. Shape affects polarity, solubility and interactions. Bond polarity and molecular shape together determine whether dipoles cancel.

A molecule's arrangement in space matters just as much as its chemical formula. Carbon dioxide and water both contain three atoms, yet carbon dioxide is linear while water is bent. Their different shapes influence whether individual bond dipoles reinforce or cancel, which in turn affects intermolecular attraction.

Imagine a tug of war with equal pulls. If the pulls point in exact opposite directions, they cancel; if they meet at an angle, the resultant need not vanish. Bond dipoles behave similarly as vectors. This is why deciding that a molecule is polar requires thinking about both bond polarity and geometry.

Electron pairs and molecular geometry

For a simple VSEPR model, count bonding regions and lone pairs around the central atom. CO₂ has two main electron regions around carbon and is linear. Water has two bonds and two lone-pair regions around oxygen, giving a bent molecular shape.

molecular polarity depends on both bond dipoles and their spatial arrangement

VSEPR is a practical model: regions of electron density around a central atom tend to arrange to reduce repulsion. Two regions often lead to a linear arrangement; three to trigonal planar; four to a tetrahedral electron-domain arrangement. Lone pairs count as regions and can alter the visible arrangement of bonded atoms.

For water, oxygen has two O–H bonding regions and two lone-pair regions. Its electron-domain geometry is approximately tetrahedral, while the molecular shape is bent. The bond dipoles do not cancel, so water has a net dipole. VSEPR is a qualitative method for many main-group molecules, not a substitute for advanced bonding models in every molecule or transition-metal complex.

Worked examples

Question. Compare CO₂ and H₂O. Which has a net molecular dipole?

Solution, step by step. CO₂ has two opposite C=O bond dipoles arranged linearly, so they cancel. Water is bent, so its O–H bond dipoles do not cancel; water is polar.

Worked example 2

Question. Why is water H₂O bent rather than linear in the common VSEPR picture?

Solution. Oxygen has two bonding pairs and two lone pairs. Four electron regions are tetrahedrally arranged; lone-pair repulsions produce a bent molecular shape (≈104.5° bond angle).

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A simple molecule has three identical bonds symmetrically arranged 120° apart in one plane and no lone pair. Would its three equal bond dipoles sum to zero?

Show worked answer

Yes; symmetry makes the vector sum of the three equal dipoles zero.

Problem 2 of 5

What is the approximate molecular shape of CO₂?

Show answer and method

Linear.

Problem 3 of 5

Is a symmetrical linear CO₂ molecule polar overall?

Show answer and method

No; bond dipoles cancel.

Problem 4 of 5

What is the common VSEPR shape of methane CH₄?

Show answer and method

Tetrahedral.

Problem 5 of 5

What is an electronegativity difference often associated with?

Show answer and method

Unequal sharing of bonding electrons and bond polarity.

Try it yourself

Build a simple ball-and-stick shape using paper or reusable modelling putty. Rotate it to see why 2D sketches can mislead.

Where this model stops

VSEPR predicts many simple main-group shapes, but transition-metal complexes and unusual bonding may need more advanced models.
