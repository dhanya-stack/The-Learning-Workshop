Optimisation Using Calculus

How do engineers choose dimensions that make something as large or as small as possible?

Finding the best value

Optimisation starts with a goal, such as maximum volume or minimum material. Constraints turn many possible designs into a manageable mathematical family. A derivative identifies a candidate peak or trough, but physical limits still matter.

Many design questions are really about finding the best possible outcome under constraints: minimum material, maximum storage, shortest travel time or lowest operating cost. The derivative helps because it tells us where a small change in a design variable stops improving the result.

But “best” must have a precise meaning. A lightweight beam might deflect too much, and a fast pump might use more power than necessary. In engineering, optimisation is not simply finding a maximum or minimum of an equation; it is deciding on an objective and identifying which values are feasible.

Critical points and derivatives

For a rectangle with fixed perimeter 20 m, if one side is x, the other is 10 − x. Its area is A(x) = x(10 − x). Differentiate: A′(x) = 10 − 2x. The stationary point occurs at x = 5.

A(x) = 10x − x²; A′(x) = 10 − 2x

If a smooth one-variable function has a local interior maximum or minimum, its derivative is often zero there. Such a stationary point is only a candidate: it could instead be a local minimum, a maximum or a flat inflection point. Check the second derivative, the sign of the first derivative, and the endpoints of the allowed interval.

For example, suppose a rectangle has a fixed perimeter P. Writing one side as x gives the other as P/2−x, so area A=x(P/2−x). Then A′=P/2−2x, and the stationary point occurs at x=P/4. Because A″=−2, this is a maximum: a square maximises area among rectangles with that perimeter. Always return the mathematical answer to the original physical constraints.

Worked examples

Question. Find the greatest area of a rectangle whose perimeter is 20 m.

Solution, step by step. Solve A′(x) = 0: 10 − 2x = 0, so x = 5 m. Then the other side is 5 m and area is 25 m². Since A″(x) = −2, this point is a maximum.

Worked example 2

Question. A rectangle has perimeter 20 m. What dimensions maximise its area?

Solution. If sides are x and 10−x, A=x(10−x). A′=10−2x=0 gives x=5; A″=−2 so it is a maximum. Square 5 m × 5 m.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. Repeat for a rectangle of perimeter 28 m. What dimensions maximise area?

Show worked answer

Each side is 7 m; the maximum area is 49 m².

Problem 2 of 5

Find stationary points of f(x)=x²−4x.

Show answer and method

2x−4=0 ⇒ x=2.

Problem 3 of 5

Is x=2 a maximum or minimum of f(x)=x²−4x?

Show answer and method

Minimum because f″(x)=2 > 0.

Problem 4 of 5

For positive x, which maximises A(x)=12x−x²?

Show answer and method

A′=12−2x=0 ⇒ x=6.

Problem 5 of 5

Why check endpoints in optimisation with a closed interval?

Show answer and method

The largest or smallest value may occur at a boundary, not a stationary point.

Try it yourself

Cut rectangles with equal perimeter from paper strips and compare the areas. Does the equal-sided case win?

Where this model stops

Stationary points need to be checked against boundaries and design constraints; not all yield maxima.
