Springs and Oscillations

What connects a guitar string, a suspension spring and a vibrating machine?

Why a displaced spring moves back

An oscillation repeatedly moves around an equilibrium position. Springs provide a restoring force that grows with displacement. With little damping, energy transfers between motion and stored elastic energy.

A spring pulled away from its equilibrium position tries to return there. Yet a mass attached to it usually does not stop exactly at equilibrium: inertia carries it past, and the spring then pulls the other way. That exchange between elastic potential energy and kinetic energy produces oscillation.

Oscillations appear in suspension systems, precision instruments, building structures and spacecraft mechanisms. Whether the motion dies away, keeps a constant amplitude or grows depends on damping and on whether external forces are supplying energy.

Restoring forces and oscillations

Hooke’s law models an ideal spring: restoring force is opposite displacement. For a mass on a spring, the natural period depends on mass and stiffness. More mass means slower oscillation; a stiffer spring means faster oscillation.

F = −kx; T = 2π√(m/k)

For an ideal linear spring, restoring force is F=−kx. With mass m and no damping, Newton’s law gives m x″+kx=0, whose angular frequency is ω=sqrt(k/m). The period is T=2πsqrt(m/k). Doubling the mass increases the period by sqrt(2), while quadrupling stiffness halves it.

Real oscillators lose energy through friction and fluid drag, so damping reduces their motion. When a repeated driving force acts near a natural frequency, resonance can produce large responses if damping is low. The simple spring equation assumes small, approximately linear deformation and a single degree of freedom; complex assemblies may have many vibration modes.

Worked examples

Question. A 0.50 kg block is attached to a spring of stiffness 200 N/m. Estimate the undamped period.

Solution, step by step. m/k = 0.50/200 = 0.0025 s². Its square root is 0.05 s. Period T = 2π × 0.05 ≈ 0.314 s.

Worked example 2

Question. A spring with k=200 N/m stretches by 0.05 m. Calculate force and elastic energy.

Solution. Force magnitude =kx=200×0.05=10 N. Stored energy=½kx²=0.5×200×0.0025=0.25 J.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A spring with k = 100 N/m is stretched 0.03 m. Find the magnitude of restoring force.

Show worked answer

|F| = kx = 100 × 0.03 = 3 N.

Problem 2 of 5

A 50 N/m spring extends 0.1 m. Restoring force magnitude?

Show answer and method

5 N.

Problem 3 of 5

How does mass affect the period of a spring oscillator T=2π√(m/k)?

Show answer and method

More mass increases the period proportional to √m.

Problem 4 of 5

What is the phase-space velocity at a turning point of ideal simple harmonic motion?

Show answer and method

Zero at the extreme displacement.

Problem 5 of 5

For ideal SHM, where is speed greatest?

Show answer and method

At equilibrium.

Try it yourself

Hang a modest mass from a suitable spring, displace slightly and time ten cycles. Divide by ten to estimate the period.

Where this model stops

The ideal model assumes small displacement, a linear spring and negligible damping. Secure suspended masses safely.
