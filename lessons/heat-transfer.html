Heat Transfer

Why does a metal spoon get hot in soup before its handle is immersed?

How heat moves from one place to another

Heat transfer is energy moving because of a temperature difference. Conduction passes energy through matter, convection transports energy between a surface and moving fluid, and radiation transfers energy by electromagnetic waves. In real situations these often occur together.

Heat moves because of temperature differences, but the path matters. A metal spoon in hot tea warms through conduction; a fan changes convection around a warm object; sunlight travels through space by radiation. In real systems, these mechanisms often operate together rather than one at a time.

Think about insulating a house or cooling electronic equipment. Thick insulation reduces conduction through walls, yet ventilation can still exchange heat through moving air. A shiny surface may have different radiative behaviour from a dull one. Understanding the dominant mechanism helps choose an effective design instead of guessing.

Conduction, convection and radiation

Steady conduction across a flat wall is proportional to conductivity k, area A and temperature difference, and inversely proportional to thickness L. Convection is often modelled using coefficient h.

Q̇conduction = kAΔT/L; Q̇convection = hAΔT

One-dimensional steady conduction through a plane slab can be approximated by Q̇=kAΔT/L, with conductivity k, area A and thickness L. Convection often uses Q̇=hA(T_surface−T_fluid), while thermal radiation depends on emissivity and the fourth power of absolute temperature.

Thermal resistances can be arranged like an electrical circuit for simple steady heat-flow paths. For series layers, the same heat rate passes through each layer while temperature drops add. This analogy is powerful but requires care: convection coefficients vary with flow conditions, temperature properties may change, and transient heating involves stored thermal energy. A heat-transfer coefficient is not a material constant.

Worked examples

Question. A wall has k = 0.8 W/(m·K), area 2 m², thickness 0.1 m and ΔT = 15 K. Find approximate steady conduction rate.

Solution, step by step. Q̇ = 0.8 × 2 × 15 / 0.1 = 240 W. A thicker wall of the same material would reduce the rate.

Worked example 2

Question. A wall has k=1 W/(m·K), area 2 m², thickness 0.1 m and 10 K temperature difference. Find steady conduction rate.

Solution. Fourier one-dimensional magnitude: Q̇=kAΔT/L=1×2×10/0.1=200 W.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A surface transfers heat with h = 10 W/(m²·K), area 0.5 m² and ΔT = 20 K. Estimate convective rate.

Show worked answer

Q̇ = 10 × 0.5 × 20 = 100 W.

Problem 2 of 5

How much heat conducts through the wall above if ΔT doubles?

Show answer and method

400 W, under constant properties and geometry.

Problem 3 of 5

What is the SI unit of thermal conductivity?

Show answer and method

W/(m·K).

Problem 4 of 5

Can heat travel through a vacuum?

Show answer and method

Yes, by thermal radiation.

Problem 5 of 5

Which heat transfer mechanism involves bulk fluid motion?

Show answer and method

Convection.

Try it yourself

Compare a metal spoon and wooden spoon in warm—not scalding—water. Explain why apparent coldness or warmth at touch differs.

Where this model stops

These are simplified steady models with prescribed properties; temperature can vary through time and space.
