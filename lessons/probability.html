Probability

How can we reason about uncertain outcomes without predicting any single trial?

Reasoning about uncertain outcomes

Probability describes how likely an outcome is in a defined model. For equally likely outcomes, it is the number of favourable cases divided by the total cases. Repeated observations may approach a stable fraction even though individual results remain unpredictable.

Probability gives a disciplined way to reason about uncertainty. When a fair die is rolled, no one knows the next result, but the possible outcomes and their chances can be described. In engineering, uncertainty may instead come from measurement noise, component variation or incomplete information, so identifying the source of randomness is important.

A probability is not a guarantee about one trial. An event with probability 0.1 can happen on the first attempt, and an event with probability 0.9 can fail. Repeated trials tend to reveal stable proportions under suitable assumptions, but small samples can look very uneven.

Counting outcomes and conditional probability

If a fair six-sided die is rolled, there are six equally likely results. The probability of an even number is 3/6 = 1/2. If two trials are independent, multiply their probabilities to find the chance both happen.

P(A) = favourable outcomes / total equally likely outcomes

If outcomes are equally likely, probability can be counted as favourable outcomes divided by all possible outcomes. More generally, probabilities must be assigned according to the model or evidence. The addition rule avoids double counting overlapping events: P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B).

Conditional probability asks how knowing one event changes our assessment of another: P(A|B)=P(A and B)/P(B), provided P(B)>0. For example, the probability that a test is positive given a fault is not the same as the probability that a fault exists given a positive test. Base rates matter, which is why a rare failure may still be unlikely after a positive diagnostic signal.

Worked examples

Question. A fair die is rolled twice. What is the probability that both results are six?

Solution, step by step. Each roll gives P(6) = 1/6. Independent rolls mean multiply: (1/6) × (1/6) = 1/36 ≈ 2.78%.

Worked example 2

Question. A bag has 3 red and 2 blue beads. Draw two without replacement. Find probability both are red.

Solution. P(red first)=3/5; P(red second | red first)=2/4. Multiply: 3/5×2/4=3/10.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. What is the chance of getting at least one head in two tosses of a fair coin?

Show worked answer

Use the complement: P(no heads) = P(two tails) = 1/4. Therefore P(at least one head) = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4.

Problem 2 of 5

What is P(even) on a fair six-sided die?

Show answer and method

3/6=1/2.

Problem 3 of 5

Two independent fair coins: P(two heads)?

Show answer and method

1/2×1/2=1/4.

Problem 4 of 5

If P(A)=0.3, find P(not A).

Show answer and method

0.7.

Problem 5 of 5

Can independent events be mutually exclusive if both have positive probability?

Show answer and method

No; independent positive-probability events can occur together.

Try it yourself

Flip a coin 30 times and record the observed fraction of heads after every five flips. Compare the run with the predicted probability.

Where this model stops

Independence and equal likelihood must be justified; do not apply simple counting blindly to a biased system.
