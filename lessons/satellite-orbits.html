How satellites orbit Earth

Understand orbit as continuous free fall and calculate orbital speed and period.

An orbit is a continuous fall

If you throw a ball horizontally, gravity curves its path toward Earth. Imagine throwing it so fast—and from high enough—that Earth curves away beneath it just as quickly. The object keeps falling around Earth rather than hitting the ground. This is the central idea of orbit.

An orbiting satellite is still pulled strongly by gravity. Astronauts experience apparent weightlessness because they and their spacecraft are falling together, not because gravity has disappeared.

A satellite stays in orbit not because gravity disappears but because gravity continually bends its motion around Earth. If you could throw an object sideways fast enough from above the atmosphere, the surface would curve away as it fell. This picture helps explain why an orbit is sustained free fall rather than suspension in space.

At low altitudes atmospheric drag eventually slows many satellites and reduces their orbital energy. Higher orbits avoid most atmospheric drag but have different periods and visibility properties. The altitude suitable for imaging Earth is not necessarily suitable for continuous communications.

Finding the speed for a circular orbit

For a circular orbit we equate gravitational acceleration to the centripetal acceleration needed to turn the satellite continuously:

GM/r² = v²/r   →   v = √(GM/r)

Earth's standard gravitational parameter μ = GM ≈ 398,600 km³/s². Distance r is measured from Earth's centre, so r = Earth radius + altitude. Use Earth radius ≈ 6371 km.

Orbital period: T = 2π √(r³/GM)

Higher circular orbits require lower orbital speed but have longer periods. This model ignores air drag, the Moon and other complications.

For an ideal circular orbit, gravity supplies the required inward acceleration: GMm/r²=mv²/r, giving v=sqrt(GM/r). Notice that spacecraft mass cancels: at a given circular orbital radius around the same central body, ideal orbital speed does not depend on spacecraft mass.

Period follows from circumference divided by orbital speed: T=2πsqrt(r³/GM). The radius r is measured from Earth's centre, not from the surface. These relations neglect perturbations such as Earth’s nonspherical gravity field, atmospheric drag and other bodies. For elliptical orbits, speed varies around the orbit, and a more general formulation uses the semi-major axis for orbital period.

Worked examples

Problem: Estimate the circular orbital speed of a satellite 400 km above Earth.
  1. Distance from Earth’s centre: r = 6371 + 400 = 6771 km.
  2. v = √(μ/r) = √(398,600 / 6771) km/s.
  3. v ≈ 7.67 km/s.

A satellite covers a huge distance even in a short time; it continuously changes the direction of its velocity.

Worked example 2

Question. Calculate orbital period for an ideal circular orbit of radius 7000 km around Earth using μ=398600 km³/s².

Solution. T=2π√(r³/μ)≈2π√(343×10⁹/398600)≈5828 s≈97.1 min.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5
Your turn: At altitude 800 km, estimate circular orbital speed with μ = 398,600 km³/s² and Earth radius 6371 km.
Show worked answer

r = 6371 + 800 = 7171 km; v = √(398,600/7171) ≈ 7.46 km/s. This is slower than a 400 km circular orbit.

Problem 2 of 5

Estimate circular speed at r=8000 km with μ=398600 km³/s².

Show answer and method

√(398600/8000)≈7.06 km/s.

Problem 3 of 5

What force keeps an unpowered satellite in circular orbit?

Show answer and method

Gravity.

Problem 4 of 5

Does a higher circular orbit have a higher or lower speed around the same body?

Show answer and method

Lower speed.

Problem 5 of 5

What is the shape of a two-body circular orbit?

Show answer and method

A circle; it is a special case of an ellipse.
