Flow Through Pipes and Pressure Losses

Why does a long narrow pipe demand more pumping effort?

Why a long pipe needs more pressure

Real fluids lose useful mechanical energy through wall friction and fittings. Narrower pipes often increase flow speed for a given flow rate, and pressure losses can rise steeply. Pipe sizing therefore trades cost, space, pressure loss and required pump power.

Water flowing through a long pipe loses mechanical energy to viscous effects. That is why a pressure difference is required to sustain flow, even when the pipe is horizontal and has constant diameter. Adding bends, valves, constrictions and fittings can increase the required pressure difference.

A useful physical picture is to compare pushing water through a drinking straw and through a wide hose. The narrow straw requires much larger pressure gradients for comparable flow. Pipe diameter often dominates pumping effort, which is why seemingly small design choices can have large operating-cost consequences.

Friction, flow rate and pressure loss

For steady flow in a straight pipe, Darcy–Weisbach relates pressure loss to friction factor f, length L, diameter D and average velocity v. The friction factor depends on Reynolds number and surface roughness.

Δp = f (L/D) (ρv²/2)

For fully developed internal flow, Darcy–Weisbach expresses a major-loss pressure drop as Δp=f_D(L/D)(ρv²/2), using the Darcy friction factor f_D. The friction factor depends on Reynolds number and relative roughness; for fully developed laminar flow in a round pipe, f_D=64/Re. Minor losses from fittings are often represented by Kρv²/2.

The system curve usually rises with flow rate because friction losses grow. A pump must supply enough head to overcome elevation changes, pressure requirements and losses. Do not confuse absolute pressure at a point with pressure drop over a component. Nor should one friction-factor correlation be used blindly in transitional flow or with strongly developing flows.

Worked examples

Question. Water flows through a 10 m pipe of diameter 0.05 m at 2 m/s. If f = 0.025 and ρ = 1000 kg/m³, find straight-pipe loss.

Solution, step by step. L/D = 10/0.05 = 200. Dynamic pressure = 1000 × 2²/2 = 2000 Pa. Δp = 0.025 × 200 × 2000 = 10,000 Pa = 10 kPa.

Worked example 2

Question. A straight-pipe system has loss proportional to Q². If loss is 12 kPa at 2 L/s, what is it at 3 L/s under same conditions?

Solution. Δp₂=12×(3/2)²=27 kPa. This uses the stated quadratic approximation.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. Same pipe but length 20 m: what straight-pipe loss results if all else is unchanged?

Show worked answer

Δp doubles to 20 kPa because L is twice as large.

Problem 2 of 5

For the same pipe and fluid, if velocity doubles in turbulent quadratic regime, loss factor?

Show answer and method

Approximately 4.

Problem 3 of 5

What does a rougher pipe often do to turbulent friction loss?

Show answer and method

Increase it, with details depending on Reynolds number.

Problem 4 of 5

Is a minor loss always numerically small?

Show answer and method

No; “minor” names fitting losses, which can dominate some systems.

Problem 5 of 5

Name two common sources of pipe pressure loss.

Show answer and method

Wall friction and valves/bends/fittings.

Try it yourself

Draw a simple loop with a pump, straight pipe, elbows and valve. Mark where energy losses occur and identify which need extra coefficients.

Where this model stops

Never confuse Darcy friction factor with Fanning friction factor; they differ by a factor of four.
