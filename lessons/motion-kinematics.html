Displacement, Velocity and Acceleration

How do you describe a moving object without yet asking what caused the motion?

Describing how something moves

Displacement is the change in position and has a direction. Distance is the length of the journey. Average velocity is displacement divided by time; speed uses distance. Acceleration measures how quickly velocity changes.

Kinematics describes motion without first asking what forces caused it. Position tells us where something is relative to an origin. Displacement is the change in position, whereas distance is the total path length travelled. Velocity adds direction to speed, and acceleration describes how velocity changes.

A car rounding a bend at constant speed still accelerates because its direction changes. Conversely, a car travelling east and slowing down has an acceleration pointing west. That is why motion is best described with vectors and graphs rather than with the word “fast” alone.

Position, velocity and acceleration

When acceleration is constant, a few useful equations relate initial velocity u, final velocity v, time t and displacement s. Sketch the direction first, then choose positive and negative signs consistently.

v = u + at; s = ut + ½at²

Average velocity equals displacement divided by elapsed time; instantaneous velocity is the derivative of position with respect to time. Acceleration is the derivative of velocity. When acceleration is constant in one dimension, equations such as v=u+at and s=ut+½at² follow by integrating these rates.

If a vehicle begins from rest and accelerates at 2 m/s² for 5 s, v=10 m/s and displacement is 25 m. These results do not apply unchanged if acceleration varies with time. On a position–time graph, slope is velocity; on a velocity–time graph, slope is acceleration and signed area is displacement. Keeping these graphical interpretations connected prevents formula memorisation from replacing understanding.

Worked examples

Question. A bicycle starts at 2 m/s and accelerates steadily at 1.5 m/s² for 4 s. Find speed and displacement.

Solution, step by step. v = 2 + 1.5 × 4 = 8 m/s. s = 2 × 4 + ½ × 1.5 × 4² = 8 + 12 = 20 m.

Worked example 2

Question. A cyclist starts from rest and accelerates at 2 m/s² for 6 s. Find final speed and distance.

Solution. v=u+at=0+2×6=12 m/s. s=ut+½at²=0+0.5×2×36=36 m.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A cart starts at rest and accelerates at 2 m/s² for 3 s. How far does it travel?

Show worked answer

s = 0 + ½ × 2 × 3² = 9 m. Its final velocity is 6 m/s.

Problem 2 of 5

Travel 100 m in 20 s at steady speed. Speed?

Show answer and method

5 m/s.

Problem 3 of 5

At constant 3 m/s² from u=4 m/s for 2 s, final velocity?

Show answer and method

10 m/s.

Problem 4 of 5

An object moves 6 m east then 2 m west. Displacement?

Show answer and method

4 m east.

Problem 5 of 5

Can speed stay constant while acceleration is nonzero?

Show answer and method

Yes, e.g. uniform circular motion changes velocity direction.

Try it yourself

Film a ball rolling down a gentle ramp. Use video timestamps and ruler marks to estimate positions, then compare successive average speeds.

Where this model stops

These equations require constant acceleration. Do not assume constant acceleration if the forcing or resistance changes.
