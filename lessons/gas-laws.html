Gases and Gas Laws

Why does a sealed balloon grow when warmed?

Why gases change volume and pressure

Gas pressure comes from particle collisions with surrounding surfaces. Volume, temperature, pressure and amount of gas are connected. Temperature must be measured on an absolute scale for the ideal-gas relation.

A gas is compressible because its particles occupy only a small fraction of the space available to them in ordinary conditions. Heating a gas increases typical molecular kinetic energies; holding the volume fixed then tends to raise pressure. Allowing the gas to expand instead can change volume and temperature together.

Balloon demonstrations show the effects dramatically, but the physical system matters. A sealed rigid can, a flexible balloon and an open container respond differently to heating because their constraints differ. Before choosing a gas law, identify what stays fixed.

Pressure, temperature and the gas laws

For an ideal gas, pV = nRT. At constant pressure and amount, V is proportional to temperature in kelvin. At constant volume, pressure is proportional to temperature. These relations approximate many gases under ordinary conditions.

pV = nRT; T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15

The ideal-gas law PV=nRT links absolute pressure, volume, amount of gas and absolute temperature. If a fixed amount of ideal gas stays at constant temperature, PV remains constant; if volume stays constant instead, pressure is proportional to kelvin temperature. Celsius must not be used directly in these proportional relationships.

Ideal-gas behaviour is approximate because real molecules have finite size and interact. It works reasonably well for many dilute gases, but deviations grow at high pressure or near condensation. In engineering, pressure quoted as gauge must be converted to absolute pressure before entering the ideal-gas equation.

Worked examples

Question. A balloon has volume 2.0 L at 300 K. If pressure and amount stay constant, what is its volume at 330 K?

Solution, step by step. V₂ = V₁T₂/T₁ = 2.0 × 330/300 = 2.2 L. The increase follows the ratio of absolute temperatures.

Worked example 2

Question. A fixed gas occupies 2 L at 100 kPa. At same temperature it is compressed to 1 L. Find pressure.

Solution. Boyle’s law p₁V₁=p₂V₂ gives p₂=100×2/1=200 kPa.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A sealed rigid container has pressure 100 kPa at 300 K. Estimate pressure at 360 K.

Show worked answer

p₂ = 100 × 360/300 = 120 kPa.

Problem 2 of 5

At constant temperature, pressure doubles. Volume becomes…?

Show answer and method

Half, for fixed gas amount.

Problem 3 of 5

Convert 27°C approximately to kelvin.

Show answer and method

300.15 K.

Problem 4 of 5

At constant pressure, raising absolute temperature from 300 to 600 K changes ideal-gas volume by what factor?

Show answer and method

It doubles.

Problem 5 of 5

What is the ideal-gas equation?

Show answer and method

pV=nRT.

Try it yourself

Watch a small, untied balloon change size in safely warm and cool water. Avoid heating a sealed rigid vessel.

Where this model stops

Real gases depart from ideal behaviour at high pressure or near condensation; never insert Celsius directly into gas-law ratios.
