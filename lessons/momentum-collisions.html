Momentum and Collisions

Why can a short collision produce a very large force?

What changes during a collision?

Momentum combines mass with velocity. In a collision, objects exert forces on each other over a brief interval, exchanging momentum. If external impulse is negligible, total system momentum stays constant, even though individual velocities change.

A moving object can be difficult to stop not only because it moves quickly but also because it has mass. Momentum combines these effects as mass times velocity. To change momentum, an interaction must exert a force over time: the accumulated effect is impulse.

A helmet or airbag does not magically remove the momentum that must change during impact. It increases the time and distance over which the person slows down, which can reduce the peak force. This makes momentum a particularly useful way of thinking about collisions where forces rise and fall very quickly.

Momentum conservation and impulse

Impulse is force integrated over time; for roughly constant force, FΔt = Δp. Spreading a momentum change over more time reduces the average force. This principle explains cushioning and crumple zones.

p = mv; J = Δp = Favg Δt

For a constant-mass particle, momentum p=mv and impulse J=∫F dt=Δp. In a closed system with negligible net external impulse, total vector momentum stays constant. Consider two skaters initially at rest who push apart: their momenta must be equal in magnitude and opposite in direction even if one skater moves faster.

Kinetic energy is not always conserved in a collision. In an inelastic collision, some becomes deformation, heat and sound, while momentum of the isolated system still balances. In a perfectly inelastic collision the bodies stick together. Conservation statements depend on the chosen system and on how much external impulse acts over the collision interval.

Worked examples

Question. A 0.20 kg ball moving at 10 m/s stops over 0.050 s. Find average force magnitude.

Solution, step by step. Initial momentum = 0.20 × 10 = 2 kg·m/s. Final momentum = 0. Change magnitude is 2 N·s. Average force magnitude = 2/0.050 = 40 N, opposing the motion.

Worked example 2

Question. A 2 kg cart at 3 m/s sticks to a stationary 1 kg cart. Find common speed, ignoring external impulses.

Solution. Momentum before =2×3+1×0=6 kg·m/s. Combined mass=3 kg. Speed after=6/3=2 m/s.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A 1 kg cart at 3 m/s joins a stationary 2 kg cart on a low-friction track. Find their shared speed.

Show worked answer

Momentum before = 1 × 3 = 3 kg·m/s. Total mass after = 3 kg. Final speed = 3/3 = 1 m/s.

Problem 2 of 5

Momentum of 5 kg moving at 4 m/s?

Show answer and method

20 kg·m/s.

Problem 3 of 5

Impulse from a 10 N constant force for 0.5 s?

Show answer and method

5 N·s.

Problem 4 of 5

When is total momentum conserved?

Show answer and method

For a system with zero net external impulse.

Problem 5 of 5

Is kinetic energy always conserved in collisions?

Show answer and method

No; it is conserved only in ideal elastic collisions.

Try it yourself

Roll two small toy carts together and compare a springy impact with one where they stick. Which quantity is always conserved approximately?

Where this model stops

Momentum conservation requires an approximately isolated system; kinetic energy is not always conserved in collisions.
