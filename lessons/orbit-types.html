Different Types of Satellite Orbits

Why might one satellite circle Earth every 90 minutes while another appears stationary?

Why satellites use different orbits

Imagine trying to watch a particular city from space. A satellite flying a few hundred kilometres above Earth can take detailed pictures, but it crosses the sky quickly. A satellite much farther away sees a larger portion of Earth at once, though fine detail is harder to resolve. Neither is the “best orbit” until we decide what the satellite must do.

An orbit is not simply a height. Three features are especially useful: its size (how far it stays from Earth), shape (nearly circular or stretched into an ellipse), and inclination (how tilted its plane is relative to the equator). These determine coverage, travel time and the ground track—the path a satellite appears to trace on a map of Earth.

Low, medium and geostationary orbits

Low Earth orbit (LEO) is usually a few hundred to roughly 2,000 km up. It offers close-up imaging, shorter radio paths and frequent trips around Earth. But one satellite moves past a location quickly, so continuous coverage generally needs several satellites.

Medium Earth orbit (MEO) lies farther out, between LEO and geosynchronous altitudes. Navigation constellations such as GPS use orbits in this broad region because they can cover large areas without being as distant as geostationary satellites.

Geostationary orbit (GEO) is a particular circular, equatorial orbit where the orbital period matches Earth's rotation relative to the stars. From the ground, that satellite appears to remain over approximately the same longitude. It is useful for communications and broad weather monitoring, but its great distance introduces radio delay and reduces suitability for very fine surface imagery.

Inclination and the Sun

A polar orbit runs nearly north–south, letting Earth rotate beneath it so the satellite can pass over many latitudes. A sun-synchronous orbit is usually highly inclined and designed so its orbital plane slowly turns, keeping each pass over a location at roughly the same local solar time. This gives more consistent lighting for comparing images. Sun-synchronous does not mean stationary relative to the Sun.

The important question is therefore not “Which orbit is highest?” but “What must we observe, how often, with what delay, and at what cost?”

Speed, period and orbital radius

Why does a low satellite go around Earth faster? Gravity supplies the inward acceleration that continually bends its direction of motion. For an ideal circular orbit, the required inward acceleration is v²/r and the gravitational acceleration is μ/r², where μ is Earth's gravitational parameter. Setting these equal gives the circular orbital speed.

v = √(μ/r)

The distance around a circle is 2πr. Dividing that distance by speed gives the time for one complete revolution:

T = 2π√(r³/μ)

Here r is distance from Earth's centre, not altitude above the ground. For a satellite at height h above a spherical Earth, use r = REarth + h. That distinction matters: adding 400 km to an orbital radius of about 6,371 km changes it much less than multiplying the radius by two.

Two conclusions follow directly: higher circular orbits have lower orbital speed but longer periods. And period changes with r to the three-halves power, not linearly. Those statements apply to ideal circular motion around the same central body.

Why “24 hours” is not quite right

Earth turns once relative to distant stars in about 23 h 56 min 4 s (one sidereal day). That is the period a geostationary satellite must match. A geosynchronous satellite has that period but may still move north–south or east–west in the sky if its orbit is inclined or elliptical. Geostationary is the narrower case: circular and equatorial.

Worked examples

Example 1 — A scaling shortcut. If the circular orbital radius around the same planet increases from r to 4r, by what factor does the period change?

Step 1. From T ∝ r3/2, write T₂/T₁ = (r₂/r₁)3/2.

Step 2. Substitute r₂/r₁ = 4: the ratio is 43/2 = (√4)3 = 8.

Result: the period becomes eight times longer. Note that this compares orbital radius, not height above Earth's surface.

Example 2 — Finding geostationary altitude. Estimate the radius and altitude of GEO. Take μ = 398,600 km³/s², one sidereal day = 86,164 s and Earth's equatorial radius = 6,378 km.

Step 1. Rearrange the period equation: r³ = μ(T/2π)².

Step 2. Substitute the values: r = [398600 × (86164/2π)²]1/3 ≈ 42,164 km from Earth's centre.

Step 3. Subtract Earth's equatorial radius: h ≈ 42,164 − 6,378 ≈ 35,786 km.

Check: the circular speed is √(398600/42164) ≈ 3.07 km/s. That speed is smaller than for a low Earth orbiter even though the travel time is much longer.

Example 3 — A mission choice. A satellite must photograph crop changes in the same region each week, ideally with similar sunlight. Would an equatorial geostationary or a sun-synchronous low Earth orbit be a more suitable starting choice?

Reasoning. For local detail, lower altitude usually helps achieve finer ground resolution for a given instrument. Repeated passes at similar local solar times also make image comparison easier.

Conclusion: a sun-synchronous LEO is a sensible starting point. But the final orbit requires trade-offs involving revisit frequency, swath width, clouds, cost and payload.

Try deciding a mission orbit

Choose an orbit for each mission: (1) a weather satellite that continuously views almost an entire hemisphere; (2) a camera that repeatedly images forests under similar lighting; (3) a navigation satellite serving large regions. Explain your selection using altitude, coverage, revisit pattern and communications delay, rather than simply naming an orbit.

Then use the altitude slider above. Compare a circular satellite near 400 km with one near 20,000 km. What changes most—speed or period? Try estimating the period ratio yourself before reading the displayed values.

What this simple calculation leaves out

The speed and period equations above assume an isolated central gravity field and a circular two-body orbit. Real satellites may follow elliptical paths, experience atmospheric drag in LEO, and undergo slow orbital changes caused by Earth's non-spherical gravity, the Sun and Moon, and small manoeuvres.

For an elliptical orbit, the period depends on the semi-major axis; the orbital speed changes along the path. Inclination affects where the satellite flies over Earth but is not determined by the circular-period formula alone.
