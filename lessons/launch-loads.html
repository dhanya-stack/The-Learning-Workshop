Launch Loads and Vibrations

What happens to delicate instruments when a launch vehicle vibrates?

What a spacecraft experiences at launch

Launch causes sustained accelerations, acoustic excitation and transient shocks. Structures must carry these loads without excessive stress, deflection or harmful resonance. A basic acceleration calculation is often the first sizing check.

A satellite launched inside a rocket experiences more than simple upward acceleration. During ascent it sees changing axial and lateral loads, aerodynamic forcing, acoustic energy, stage events and vibration transmitted through its mounting structure. Later, deployment devices may introduce their own short shocks.

This matters for delicate equipment and fastened joints. A component might survive the average acceleration but fail because a structural natural frequency lies near a strong vibration input. Mechanical design must consider how loads travel through the entire assembly, not only the mass of the payload.

Acceleration, vibration and structural loads

An instrument subject to acceleration a imposes an inertial load ma on its mounting. Structural dynamics adds stiffness, damping and natural frequencies; a system excited near resonance can respond more strongly than a static model predicts.

F_inertial = ma; f_n = (1/2π)√(k/m) for an ideal spring–mass model

A simple inertial load estimate uses F=ma, but actual launch qualification often requires multiple static-equivalent load cases, random vibration spectra, acoustic exposure and shock environments specified by the launcher. Modal analysis identifies natural frequencies and shapes; forced-response analysis estimates response to excitation.

If a 10 kg payload experiences a net specified acceleration of 6g along one axis, its inertial loading magnitude is about 10×6×9.81≈589 N. That arithmetic does not locate stress hotspots or cover dynamic amplification. Mounting stiffness, preload, interfaces, test fixtures and acceptance criteria all need analysis and verification.

Worked examples

Question. A 4 kg payload experiences an axial acceleration of 6g. Find the corresponding inertial-force magnitude.

Solution, step by step. a = 6 × 9.81 = 58.86 m/s². Force magnitude F = 4 × 58.86 = 235.44 N. A real design must consider load directions and margins.

Worked example 2

Question. A 5 kg component experiences axial acceleration 8g with g=9.81 m/s². Find inertial force magnitude in a static-equivalent estimate.

Solution. F=ma=5×8×9.81≈392.4 N. Load direction and mount geometry must also be checked.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A 2 kg unit sees 5g acceleration. Estimate inertial load.

Show worked answer

F = 2 × 5 × 9.81 = 98.1 N.

Problem 2 of 5

A structure vibrates at 50 Hz. What is the period of one oscillation?

Show answer and method

T = 1/f = 1/50 = 0.020 s, or 20 ms.

Problem 3 of 5

Why is resonance important for launch structures?

Show answer and method

Excitation near a natural frequency can magnify dynamic response.

Problem 4 of 5

What units describe vibration frequency?

Show answer and method

Hertz (Hz).

Problem 5 of 5

What test is used to assess survival of launch vibration?

Show answer and method

Commonly sine and random-vibration testing to mission-specific levels.

Try it yourself

Set a small object on a flexible ruler and tap lightly. What changes its vibration frequency: more mass or a stiffer ruler?

Where this model stops

Do not treat a launch vibration environment as equivalent to a single static 6g load; dynamic qualification needs spectra and test data.
