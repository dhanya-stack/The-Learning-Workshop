Powering a satellite

Can sunlight run a spacecraft? Build a simple solar-power budget and estimate how much energy a battery must store during eclipse.

A spacecraft needs an energy budget

A satellite may need electrical power for its radio, computer, instruments, heaters and pointing systems. Solar panels generate electricity when they receive sunlight; batteries supply energy when the spacecraft is in Earth's shadow.

Engineers therefore ask two different questions: Is enough power available while illuminated? And is there enough stored energy during eclipse? The power budget is a starting point for answering both.

A satellite needs energy to operate its computer, communications link, sensors, heaters and actuators. Many Earth-orbiting spacecraft use solar arrays during sunlight and rechargeable batteries during eclipse, when Earth blocks direct sunlight. Power planning therefore concerns both the instantaneous loads and the time pattern of generation and storage.

Imagine a camera that draws a large amount of power only when taking and transmitting images. A simple average-power figure might hide a short peak that the electrical system must support. Design must consider peak demand, battery charge state, available sunlight, conversion efficiency and end-of-life degradation.

From sunlight to electrical power

Near Earth, sunlight just outside the atmosphere delivers roughly 1361 W/m² onto a surface facing the Sun. Real solar panels capture only a fraction. In this deliberately simple example, an orientation factor of 0.8 accounts for not facing the Sun perfectly; another factor of 0.9 approximates electrical losses.

Solar output ≈ 1361 × panel area × efficiency × 0.8 × 0.9 (W)

For a 30-minute eclipse at steady power demand P, the spacecraft must draw E = P × 0.5 hours from storage. If we plan to use at most 80% of a battery's nominal stored energy, its simplified capacity requirement is E / 0.8 in Wh.

Important: Real spacecraft design also considers degradation, temperatures, duty cycles, sunlight duration, battery charging efficiency, safety margins and mission orbit. These numbers are only an educational first-pass estimate.

A basic energy budget integrates power over time: E=∫P(t)dt. A 20 W load operated for two hours needs 40 Wh, before electrical losses. A battery that nominally stores 100 Wh may not deliver all of it within allowable depth-of-discharge, temperature and ageing constraints.

Solar-array output depends on incident sunlight, effective area, cell efficiency, orientation, temperature and degradation. During an eclipse, stored energy must meet the loads; during sunlit portions, arrays must both serve current demands and replenish storage. A practical power budget uses duty cycles and margins rather than assuming every subsystem draws its maximum continuously.

Worked examples

Problem: A satellite has 1 m² of panels at 25% efficiency, with the orientation/loss factors above. Its electrical load is 200 W. Does its solar generation exceed its load?
  1. Output ≈ 1361 × 1 × 0.25 × 0.8 × 0.9 = 245 W.
  2. Surplus while illuminated ≈ 245 − 200 = 45 W.
  3. For a 30-minute eclipse, the load needs 200 × 0.5 = 100 Wh of usable energy. At 80% allowable discharge, nominal battery capacity must be at least 125 Wh.

Having a positive power surplus is necessary but not sufficient: the sunlit portion of the orbit must be long enough to recharge the energy used in eclipse.

Worked example 2

Question. A spacecraft draws 150 W over a 40-minute eclipse. Find usable energy and 75%-depth-of-discharge nominal capacity.

Solution. E=150×(40/60)=100 Wh. Nominal capacity≥100/0.75≈133.3 Wh, before efficiency and margin.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5
Your turn: A satellite draws 120 W for a 30-minute eclipse. How many usable Wh are needed? What nominal battery capacity does 80% allowable discharge suggest?
Show worked answer

Usable energy = 120 × 0.5 = 60 Wh. Nominal capacity ≥ 60/0.8 = 75 Wh, before additional engineering margins and losses.

Problem 2 of 5

A 60 W load runs for 2 hours: energy?

Show answer and method

120 Wh.

Problem 3 of 5

A panel of 2 m² at 20% efficiency receives 1000 W/m² normally. Ideal electric output?

Show answer and method

1000×2×0.2=400 W.

Problem 4 of 5

If panels make 200 W and load is 250 W, instantaneous deficit?

Show answer and method

50 W.

Problem 5 of 5

Why does a spacecraft need a battery even with solar arrays?

Show answer and method

To operate during eclipse and handle loads that exceed instantaneous solar production.

Try it yourself

On paper, list five things a small satellite might need to power. Assign an estimated load and time-on percentage to each; compute its average load. Later we can turn that table into a spreadsheet or a Python tool.

Think further: Would a spacecraft in deep space have the same solar power available as one near Earth?
