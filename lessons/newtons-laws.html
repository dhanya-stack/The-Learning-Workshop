Newton's laws of motion

Understand why objects speed up, slow down, or keep moving—and solve a force problem step by step.

Three laws, one way of thinking

First law: an object maintains its velocity unless an external net force acts on it. Being in motion does not require a force; changing motion does.

Second law: a net force produces acceleration. The same net force gives a smaller acceleration to a larger mass.

ΣF = ma   (in an inertial reference frame)

Third law: when one object pushes another, the second pushes back with an equal and opposite force. These forces act on different objects, so they do not cancel when analysing a single object.

Newton’s laws describe how motion changes when bodies interact. A force is not required to keep an object moving at constant velocity in a straight line; it is required to change that velocity. This is often counterintuitive because on Earth friction and drag continually slow objects unless something pushes them.

The first law identifies inertia, the second law quantifies acceleration and the third law describes interaction pairs. Think of a passenger in a braking bus: the passenger continues moving forward relative to the bus unless a seat belt or seat supplies a force. Nothing has to “push” the passenger forward; their existing motion tends to continue.

Draw the forces first

Imagine pushing a cart. Gravity acts downward, the floor's normal force acts upward, and your push and friction act horizontally. The cart's horizontal acceleration depends on the difference between those horizontal forces.

If a 14 N push acts right and friction is 5 N left, the horizontal net force is 9 N right—not 14 N.

Newton’s second law for constant mass is the vector relationship ΣF=ma. It concerns the net external force on a chosen object, not one convenient applied force. If you push a 5 kg trolley with 20 N forward and friction supplies 5 N backward, the net horizontal force is 15 N and the acceleration is 3 m/s² forward.

The third law is frequently misunderstood: equal and opposite forces act on different interacting bodies, so they do not cancel in one object's free-body diagram. The Earth pulls a falling ball downward while the ball pulls the Earth upward with an equal force; the accelerations differ because the masses differ enormously. State your object, coordinate directions and assumptions before applying the equations.

Worked examples

Problem: A 2 kg trolley is pushed with 12 N to the right while friction is 4 N to the left. Find its acceleration.
  1. Net horizontal force = 12 − 4 = 8 N to the right.
  2. Use ΣF = ma. Rearrange: a = ΣF/m.
  3. a = 8/2 = 4 m/s² to the right.

The normal force and weight balance vertically because the cart has no vertical acceleration.

Worked example 2

Question. A 4 kg trolley feels 30 N forward and 6 N resistance. Find its acceleration.

Solution. Net force = 30−6=24 N. By Newton’s second law, a=24/4=6 m/s² forward.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5
Your turn: A 5 kg box experiences 25 N east and 10 N west. Find its acceleration.
Show worked answer

ΣF = 25 − 10 = 15 N east. a = 15/5 = 3 m/s² east.

Problem 2 of 5

A 3 kg object accelerates at 2 m/s². What is its net force?

Show answer and method

F=ma=6 N.

Problem 3 of 5

What is acceleration of a 10 kg object with net force zero?

Show answer and method

0 m/s².

Problem 4 of 5

If equal opposing forces act on a stationary box, must it accelerate?

Show answer and method

No: the net force is zero.

Problem 5 of 5

Why does a person move forward when a bus brakes suddenly?

Show answer and method

Inertia: the body tends to keep its prior velocity until a force changes it.

Try it yourself

Roll a toy car on a smooth floor, then on a rough cloth. Observe how quickly it slows. Friction changes its velocity even after your hand stops pushing.

Think further: When you push a wall, why do you feel it pushing back? Use Newton's third law to name the two interacting objects.
