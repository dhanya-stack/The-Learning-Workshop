Thermal Management in Space

Why can a spacecraft overheat in sunlight yet become too cold in shadow?

Managing temperature without air

In vacuum there is almost no surrounding fluid to carry heat away by convection. Spacecraft exchange energy mainly by radiation and internally by conduction. Surfaces absorb sunlight and emit infrared energy; temperature follows their balance.

On Earth, a fan can carry heat away through air. A spacecraft in vacuum has essentially no external convective cooling, so it must conduct heat internally and radiate energy from suitable surfaces. Solar illumination, planetary infrared radiation, reflected sunlight and electronic equipment all contribute to its thermal balance.

The same spacecraft may alternate between intense sunlight and Earth's shadow. Some components overheat in one condition while others become too cold in another. Thermal design therefore includes radiators, insulation, heaters and conductive paths instead of relying on one universal cooling device.

Radiation and spacecraft heat balance

For an idealised surface, thermal radiation scales with absolute temperature to the fourth power. Surface emissivity, area and temperature set radiated power. Solar absorptivity describes how much incoming sunlight is absorbed.

Q̇radiated = εσAT⁴ (to cold surroundings)

A surface radiates approximately Q̇=εσA(T⁴−T_surroundings⁴) for an idealised view of an effective radiation sink, with absolute temperatures in kelvin. The fourth-power dependence means temperature strongly affects radiative power. A surface's solar absorptivity and infrared emissivity are distinct properties; a coating can absorb little sunlight while radiating heat effectively.

A real spacecraft has multiple surfaces exchanging radiation with each other, Earth and deep space, so view factors matter. Transient thermal analysis tracks heat capacity through eclipse transitions. A single formula is a useful first estimate but cannot replace detailed geometry, orbit conditions and worst-case hot/cold analyses.

Worked examples

Question. A surface of area 1 m², emissivity 0.8 and temperature 300 K radiates to deep space. Take σ = 5.67×10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴). Estimate power.

Solution, step by step. 300⁴ = 8.1×10⁹ K⁴. Q̇ = 0.8 × 5.67×10⁻⁸ × 1 × 8.1×10⁹ ≈ 367 W, ignoring absorbed radiation.

Worked example 2

Question. A spacecraft absorbs 80 W and dissipates 120 W internally. At steady state, how much heat must it radiate if there are no other energy flows?

Solution. Thermal balance: emitted power=80+120=200 W. Real temperatures depend on emissivity, areas, orientation and view factors.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. At the same emissivity and area, by what factor does radiation increase if temperature doubles in kelvin?

Show worked answer

T⁴ scaling gives 2⁴ = 16 times greater emitted power.

Problem 2 of 5

Can a hot spacecraft lose heat by ordinary air convection in vacuum?

Show answer and method

No; thermal radiation and conduction through contact paths dominate.

Problem 3 of 5

What are units of absolute temperature for radiative calculations?

Show answer and method

Kelvin.

Problem 4 of 5

If an ideal blackbody temperature doubles, emitted power per area scales by what factor?

Show answer and method

16, from Stefan–Boltzmann T⁴.

Problem 5 of 5

What does multilayer insulation mainly suppress?

Show answer and method

Radiative heat exchange between surfaces.

Try it yourself

Make a paper thermal balance with incoming sunlight, internal electronics heat and outgoing radiation for a small spacecraft.

Where this model stops

A spacecraft does not radiate to an empty zero-temperature sink everywhere; view factors and planetary infrared matter.
