Satellite Communications and Antennas

How can a tiny spacecraft send information back to a distant antenna?

How spacecraft exchange information

Communication is an energy-and-information link. A transmitter radiates electromagnetic waves, an antenna directs some of that energy, and a receiver detects a weak signal amid noise. Greater distance usually means greater spreading loss.

A satellite communication link carries information through electromagnetic waves between transmitters and receivers. The signal becomes weaker with distance because energy spreads through space, and receivers must detect it against noise and interference. Antenna direction, available power and data rate therefore become design constraints.

Imagine sending one image from a low Earth orbit satellite during a brief ground-station pass. High data rate is useful, but the satellite may only see that station for minutes. A system must plan both how strongly it can transmit and how long it can maintain a usable connection.

Signals, antennas and link budgets

In free space, received power decreases with distance squared for fixed transmitter and antenna characteristics. Data rate, bandwidth, pointing accuracy and atmospheric effects all influence the link budget.

free-space spreading: received power ∝ 1/R²

For an ideal free-space link, path loss in decibels is L_fs=20log₁₀(4πR/λ), where R is range and λ is wavelength. The full link budget combines transmitter power, antenna gains, propagation losses, pointing losses and receiver sensitivity. Greater range increases loss, while antenna directivity can concentrate power toward the intended direction.

Antenna gain is not extra generated energy; it describes how radiation is distributed spatially. Wider bandwidth and higher data rates can demand more received signal quality depending on modulation and coding. Ground-station geometry, atmosphere, regulatory spectrum constraints and redundancy all affect actual mission design.

Worked examples

Question. Two otherwise identical free-space links operate at distances R and 2R. What happens to received power?

Solution, step by step. Increasing range by factor 2 reduces received power to 1/2² = 1/4. In decibels, this is about 6 dB more path loss.

Worked example 2

Question. A radio signal travels 36,000 km one way in vacuum. Estimate delay using c=300,000 km/s.

Solution. t=d/c=36000/300000=0.12 s one way; ignore processing and routing delays.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. If range triples, what fraction of received power remains in this simplified model?

Show worked answer

1/3² = 1/9 of the original received power.

Problem 2 of 5

Time for light to travel 300 km in vacuum at 300000 km/s?

Show answer and method

0.001 s = 1 ms.

Problem 3 of 5

If an antenna gain increases, does the radiated energy necessarily increase?

Show answer and method

Not necessarily; gain describes directionality for the same total radiated power.

Problem 4 of 5

Why use a directional high-gain antenna?

Show answer and method

To concentrate signal and improve link budget in a chosen direction.

Problem 5 of 5

What causes free-space signal strength to weaken with increasing range?

Show answer and method

Energy spreads across increasing spherical area; received power decreases with range squared under ideal free-space assumptions.

Try it yourself

Compare a broad flashlight beam with a narrow one as an analogy for antenna directionality. Note how pointing precision becomes important.

Where this model stops

Antenna gain, frequency, atmosphere and receiver noise matter; distance-squared behaviour is only one part of a link budget.
