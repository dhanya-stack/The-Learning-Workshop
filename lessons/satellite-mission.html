Designing a Simple Satellite Mission

Where should a satellite design begin: with hardware, orbit or an actual question?

What must a satellite accomplish?

Start with a mission: what information or service is needed, for whom, and how often? Requirements follow from that purpose. Only then choose orbit, payload, subsystems and budget. This keeps attractive hardware from becoming a solution in search of a problem.

A successful satellite begins with a question, not a shopping list of components. Do we want to photograph coastal erosion, measure atmospheric temperature or relay emergency messages? The mission objective determines what must be measured, how often, from where and with what accuracy.

From that objective follow requirements for payload, orbit, pointing, data handling, power, thermal control and communications. Choosing an orbit before defining the mission can leave the instrument with the wrong coverage or resolution. Good design starts by making vague ambitions measurable.

From mission requirements to system design

Mission concepts can be assessed with a simple table containing payload resolution or coverage, revisit time, lifetime, mass, average power and communications demand. Requirements must be measurable enough to test.

average power = Σ(load power × duty-cycle fraction)

Translate the mission statement into testable requirements. For example, “monitor a river basin” becomes a target ground coverage area, required spatial resolution, revisit interval, data latency and operating lifetime. Each requirement then maps to subsystem capabilities and a method for verification by analysis, inspection or test.

Build initial mass, power, volume and data budgets with explicit margins. Trade studies compare plausible alternatives rather than assuming one configuration is uniquely best. As the design matures, track interfaces and risks: a communications upgrade may increase power draw and radiator needs, while a higher-resolution instrument may demand better pointing. The mission succeeds as an integrated system, not as separate well-designed parts.

Worked examples

Question. A payload uses 20 W for 30% of the time; computer 5 W continuously; radio 10 W for 10%. Find average demand.

Solution, step by step. Average payload = 20 × 0.30 = 6 W; computer = 5 W; radio = 10 × 0.10 = 1 W. Total average demand = 6 + 5 + 1 = 12 W, before margins.

Worked example 2

Question. A hypothetical satellite needs 80 W payload power for 25% of each day, plus 20 W continuously. Find average load.

Solution. Average payload=80×0.25=20 W; add 20 W constant load → average total=40 W. Peak when payload active=100 W.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A sensor uses 15 W for 40% of the time and a computer draws 4 W continuously. Find average total.

Show worked answer

15 × 0.40 + 4 = 10 W.

Problem 2 of 5

A 60 kg satellite has a 10% system margin. Budgeted mass?

Show answer and method

66 kg.

Problem 3 of 5

List two measurable mission requirements for an Earth-observation satellite.

Show answer and method

Examples: ground resolution and revisit time, stated quantitatively.

Problem 4 of 5

Why define orbit before finalising a communications link?

Show answer and method

Range and contact duration influence antenna, power and data-return requirements.

Problem 5 of 5

Which is a requirement and which is a solution: “image every 12 h” versus “use a sun-synchronous orbit”?

Show answer and method

“Image every 12 h” is a requirement; orbit selection is a proposed solution.

Try it yourself

Write a one-page concept for a weather or environmental observation satellite: mission question, three requirements and a first subsystem budget.

Where this model stops

Average power is not peak power. A mission also needs link availability, energy storage and credible subsystem margins.
