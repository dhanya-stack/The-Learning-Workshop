How rockets work

Discover thrust, exhaust momentum, gravity and the simplest rocket calculations.

How can a rocket push itself in empty space?

A rocket does not need to push on air. It carries propellant and accelerates exhaust mass backward. The rocket gains forward momentum as the exhaust carries momentum in the opposite direction.

Newton's third law gives a useful intuition, while conservation of momentum provides the quantitative picture. A simplified thrust relationship is:

Thrust ≈ ṁ vₑ

Here ṁ (kg/s) is the propellant mass flow rate and vₑ (m/s) is effective exhaust velocity. Real engines can also have a pressure-thrust contribution. Rockets lose mass as propellant is used; this first lesson treats mass as fixed for a short instant.

A rocket does not need to push against air to move. It accelerates by expelling mass in one direction and receiving an opposite momentum change. That is why a rocket can work in vacuum, and why its exhaust direction matters even when its surroundings contain no gas.

A balloon rocket sliding along a string demonstrates the same principle at a simple level: air leaves the opening and the balloon moves oppositely. A real rocket adds controlled combustion or another propulsion process, a nozzle, tanks, guidance, structures and safety systems. The underlying conservation of momentum is simple; achieving a reliable flight vehicle is not.

Thrust is not the same as acceleration

At Earth’s surface a vertical rocket has weight mg downward. If we ignore air resistance for a moment, its upward acceleration at an instant is approximately (T − mg)/m, where T is thrust.

If thrust equals weight, acceleration is zero at that instant. If thrust exceeds weight, the rocket accelerates upward in this simple model.

A common ideal thrust relationship is F=ṁv_e+(p_e−p_a)A_e, where ṁ is exhaust mass flow, v_e the exit speed relative to the rocket, and the second term accounts for pressure differences at the nozzle exit. Thrust changes the rocket's momentum, while aerodynamic drag and gravity also act during atmospheric ascent.

A large thrust does not automatically mean an efficient rocket. Specific impulse describes how effectively propellant produces impulse, and vehicle mass changes as fuel is consumed. A rocket's mission also depends on stability, mass fraction, guidance and trajectory. When comparing propulsion technologies, distinguish thrust (force), total impulse (force integrated over time) and energy required.

Explore thrust and weight

An idealized rocket at liftoff

Thrust 2000 N; weight 1471.5 N; acceleration 3.52 m/s².

Ignore aerodynamic drag, pressure-thrust correction and changing mass. No engine design or propellant-making instructions.

Worked examples

Problem: A rocket has mass 150 kg at one instant. It ejects 2 kg/s with effective exhaust speed 1000 m/s. Estimate upward acceleration near Earth’s surface.
  1. Idealized thrust: ṁ vₑ = 2 × 1000 = 2000 N.
  2. Weight: mg = 150 × 9.81 = 1471.5 N.
  3. Net upward force = 2000 − 1471.5 = 528.5 N.
  4. Acceleration = 528.5/150 = 3.52 m/s² upward.

A real trajectory needs air drag, variable mass and changing gravitational acceleration.

Worked example 2

Question. An ideal rocket expels 1.5 kg/s at effective exhaust speed 2000 m/s. Estimate thrust, ignoring pressure correction.

Solution. Thrust ≈ ṁve = 1.5×2000=3000 N. It acts opposite to exhaust momentum flow.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5
Your turn: A 100 kg idealized rocket generates 1500 N upward thrust on Earth. Neglect drag. What is its initial upward acceleration?
Show worked answer

Weight = 981 N. Net force = 1500 − 981 = 519 N. a = 519/100 = 5.19 m/s² upward.

Problem 2 of 5

At ṁ=2 kg/s and ve=1500 m/s, ideal thrust?

Show answer and method

3000 N.

Problem 3 of 5

Why can a rocket accelerate in vacuum?

Show answer and method

It expels reaction mass, conserving momentum; it does not need surrounding air.

Problem 4 of 5

A 200 kg vehicle experiences net upward force 400 N. Acceleration?

Show answer and method

400/200=2 m/s² upward.

Problem 5 of 5

If rocket mass drops while thrust stays constant, what happens to thrust/mass acceleration?

Show answer and method

It increases, with other forces treated separately.

Try it safely with a balloon

Thread a string through a drinking straw and pull the string taut across a room. Tape an inflated, untied balloon to the straw, with the opening pointing backward. Release it and observe motion in the opposite direction to the escaping air.

Extend it: Does a larger balloon always travel farther? What else changes—drag, mass, nozzle direction or friction? This is a demonstration of the principle, not a model of an actual rocket engine.
