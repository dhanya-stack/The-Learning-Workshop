Work and conservation of energy

Follow energy as an object descends: gravitational potential energy becomes kinetic energy in an ideal, frictionless model.

Energy changes form

Hold a ball above the floor. It has gravitational potential energy because of its position relative to the floor. Release it, and its speed increases: potential energy becomes kinetic energy. The total mechanical energy remains constant if we neglect air resistance and other losses.

This is often an easier way to solve motion problems than calculating acceleration at every moment. Real systems can transfer mechanical energy into heat, sound and deformation; total energy is still conserved.

Energy is a way of accounting for a system's ability to produce changes. A lifted book, a moving bicycle and a compressed spring store energy in different forms. Work transfers energy through a force acting over displacement, while heat transfers energy because of temperature difference.

The value of an energy approach is that we do not need to follow every instant of motion to compare initial and final states. A skateboard rolling down a hill trades gravitational potential energy for kinetic energy, but some mechanical energy also becomes internal energy through friction. “Energy is conserved” does not mean useful mechanical energy stays constant.

Two useful equations

Gravitational potential energy U = mgh
Kinetic energy K = ½mv²

Here m is mass (kg), h is height above the chosen reference (m), g ≈ 9.81 m/s² near Earth's surface, and v is speed (m/s).

An object released from rest at height H has initial mechanical energy mgH. At a lower height h, mgh + ½mv² = mgH. Rearranging gives v = √[2g(H − h)]. The mass cancels: the speed in this ideal model does not depend on mass.

For a constant force acting along a displacement, work W=Fd; more generally W is the integral of the force component along the path. Kinetic energy is K=½mv², and the net work on a particle equals its change in kinetic energy. Near Earth's surface, gravitational potential energy changes by mgΔh.

If a 2 kg object falls through 3 m from rest without drag, the potential-energy loss is 2×9.81×3≈58.9 J. That becomes kinetic energy, giving v=sqrt(2gh)≈7.67 m/s. If drag is significant, the final speed is lower while total energy remains conserved across a larger system including the surrounding air and thermal effects. Set system boundaries clearly before writing conservation equations.

Worked examples

Problem: A 2 kg cart starts from rest 5 m above a reference level and rolls along an ideal frictionless track. What is its speed at height 1.8 m?
  1. Height lost: Δh = 5 − 1.8 = 3.2 m.
  2. Use ½mv² = mgΔh, cancelling m: v = √(2gΔh).
  3. v = √(2 × 9.81 × 3.2) ≈ 7.92 m/s.

On a real track, rolling rotation and friction can change the result. Our ideal model treats the cart as a particle with translational kinetic energy only.

Worked example 2

Question. A 1.5 kg cart moves at 4 m/s. Find its kinetic energy.

Solution. Eₖ=(1/2)mv²=0.5×1.5×16=12 J.

Try it yourself

Roll a small ball down a low, stable cardboard ramp and compare how far it travels when released from two different heights. Keep the setup near the ground and away from edges. Note that the ball also has rotational kinetic energy, so the point-mass speed formula above is only an approximation.

Think further: Where does mechanical energy go when the ball stops?
