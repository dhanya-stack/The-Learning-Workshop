Bernoulli’s Principle

How are fluid pressure and speed connected along a flow?

Pressure and speed in a moving fluid

A flowing fluid carries mechanical energy in pressure, movement and elevation. Under ideal conditions, increasing velocity along a streamline can be accompanied by a decrease in static pressure. The energy balance is more useful than a slogan that fast flow always has low pressure.

When fluid accelerates through a narrowing passage, its pressure and kinetic energy can change together. Bernoulli’s equation helps track these changes along a streamline when the flow assumptions permit it. The familiar claim that faster flow “always means lower pressure” is too broad: pumps, friction, height changes and different streamline conditions can alter the relationship.

An instructive question is where the energy comes from. If a fluid travels at the same height and gains speed in an ideal unpowered passage, pressure energy can be converted into kinetic energy. In a real pipe, some mechanical energy also becomes internal energy through viscous losses.

Following energy along a streamline

For steady, incompressible, inviscid flow along a streamline with no added or extracted shaft work, Bernoulli’s equation relates pressure p, density ρ, speed v and elevation z. Real flows need loss terms.

p + ½ρv² + ρgz = constant

For steady, incompressible, inviscid flow along a streamline without shaft work, p+½ρv²+ρgz is constant. Each term has units of pressure: static pressure, dynamic pressure and elevation-related pressure. In a horizontal ideal nozzle, the elevation term does not change, so an increase in speed corresponds to a decrease in static pressure.

For a real system, introduce a pressure-loss or head-loss term rather than forcing the ideal equation to fit measured data. A Pitot tube uses a pressure difference to estimate speed under appropriate assumptions, while a pump adds mechanical energy to the fluid. Pressure differences also drive acceleration; interpreting cause and effect requires momentum as well as energy conservation.

Worked examples

Question. Water flows horizontally from 2 m/s to 4 m/s without losses. What is p₂ − p₁ if ρ = 1000 kg/m³?

Solution, step by step. Elevation cancels. p₂ − p₁ = ½ρ(v₁² − v₂²) = 500(4 − 16) = −6000 Pa. Pressure drops by 6 kPa in this ideal model.

Worked example 2

Question. Water flows horizontally: speed rises from 2 to 6 m/s. Neglect losses; ρ=1000 kg/m³. Find pressure change.

Solution. Bernoulli: p₂−p₁=½ρ(v₁²−v₂²)=500(4−36)=−16,000 Pa. Pressure falls by 16 kPa.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. At equal height, water accelerates from 1 to 3 m/s. Estimate pressure change, neglecting losses.

Show worked answer

Δp = ½ × 1000 × (1² − 3²) = −4000 Pa.

Problem 2 of 5

For ideal level flow, speed increases. Static pressure tends to…?

Show answer and method

Decrease.

Problem 3 of 5

Write Bernoulli in head form between two points.

Show answer and method

p/(ρg) + v²/(2g) + z = constant along a streamline under its assumptions.

Problem 4 of 5

Does Bernoulli in its simplest form include pipe friction?

Show answer and method

No; loss terms must be added.

Problem 5 of 5

Can Bernoulli be used without checking flow conditions?

Show answer and method

No; assumptions such as steady, negligible viscous loss, incompressible along a streamline need checking.

Try it yourself

Draw pressure and velocity labels at two pipe sections. Explain which quantities must be measured to check the ideal prediction.

Where this model stops

Do not apply Bernoulli without checking its assumptions or ignoring viscous losses, pumps and turbulence.
