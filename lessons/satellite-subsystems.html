Anatomy of a Satellite

How can a satellite be understood as a set of interacting functions?

A satellite is a connected system

A satellite is a system, not just a box of instruments. It needs a mission payload plus platform functions such as power, structure, attitude control, thermal regulation, communications and onboard computing. Interfaces link them.

A satellite is not simply a camera placed in orbit. It is a coordinated collection of systems that must generate power, control temperature, know and adjust its orientation, communicate, process commands and survive launch. Payload instruments provide the mission data; the supporting bus keeps them usable.

Requirements create dependencies. A more powerful transmitter may require a larger power system and additional heat rejection. A camera requiring long exposures may demand better pointing stability. Thinking in terms of connected subsystems prevents a design change in one area from creating unexpected problems elsewhere.

How the subsystems work together

A useful first design technique is an allocation table: for each subsystem record its purpose, average and peak power, mass, data rate and major interface. Budgets are constrained; increasing one subsystem’s demand affects others.

total system mass = Σ subsystem masses + margins

A common spacecraft breakdown includes structures and mechanisms, electrical power, thermal control, command and data handling, communications, attitude determination and control, propulsion where required, and the payload. Interfaces connect them through mass, volume, electrical power, data rates, heat flows and mechanical loads.

A mass budget, power budget and data budget translate concepts into testable constraints. Suppose an imaging instrument produces 2 GB per day. The communications link must transfer that data within available ground-station passes, while storage must accommodate delays. A systems-engineering approach records assumptions, margins and verification plans rather than treating each subsystem in isolation.

Worked examples

Question. A concept satellite has 4 kg payload, 3 kg structure, 2 kg power subsystem, 1 kg computer and 2 kg other equipment. Find subtotal and 20% margin estimate.

Solution, step by step. Subtotal = 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 2 = 12 kg. Adding 20% of subtotal gives estimated design mass 12 × 1.20 = 14.4 kg.

Worked example 2

Question. A spacecraft masses 12 kg structure, 5 kg payload, 3 kg battery and 2 kg avionics. Find subtotal and margin at 20% of subtotal.

Solution. Subtotal=12+5+3+2=22 kg. Margin=0.20×22=4.4 kg. Total including margin=26.4 kg.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. Subsystem subtotal is 8 kg and concept-stage reserve is 25%. What total allocation do you plan?

Show worked answer

8 × 1.25 = 10 kg.

Problem 2 of 5

What subsystem maintains attitude?

Show answer and method

Attitude determination and control (ADCS).

Problem 3 of 5

What subsystem sends telemetry to ground?

Show answer and method

Communications.

Problem 4 of 5

If five subsystems each need 2 W continuously, what is total ideal load?

Show answer and method

10 W.

Problem 5 of 5

Why is a system mass budget maintained?

Show answer and method

To track total mass, allocations and margins against launch constraints.

Try it yourself

Sketch a block diagram with payload in the centre and arrows showing power, data and mechanical interfaces to the bus.

Where this model stops

A budget margin is not a substitute for actual design maturity, and one contingency figure rarely fits every subsystem.
