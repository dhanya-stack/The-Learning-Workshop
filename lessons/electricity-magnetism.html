Electricity and Magnetism

How does a battery create current, and why can a magnet move a motor?

Charges, currents and fields

Electric potential difference is energy per unit charge. Current is the rate of moving charge, and resistance tells us how strongly a component opposes current. Changing magnetic fields and electric currents are linked, enabling motors and generators.

Electricity begins with charge and its interactions. An electric field describes the force that a small test charge would experience at a point; a voltage difference describes energy transferred per unit charge between points. These are related ideas, not interchangeable terms.

Moving charge makes electric current, and currents generate magnetic fields. Magnetism also influences moving charges, allowing motors to produce torque and generators to produce electrical energy. Many devices combine both effects: fans, sensors, wireless communication and power supplies all rely on electromagnetic principles.

How electrical and magnetic forces relate

Ohm’s law describes an ideal resistor: voltage equals current times resistance. Electrical power delivered to that resistor is voltage times current. Magnetic force on a current-carrying conductor depends on current, field and orientation.

V = IR; P = VI

Ohm’s law V=IR describes the voltage–current relation of an ideal resistor at fixed conditions. For a 6 Ω resistor connected across 12 V, current is 2 A and dissipated power is VI=24 W. Real components can be nonlinear or temperature dependent, and a circuit's current depends on the full network.

A changing magnetic flux through a circuit induces an electromotive force, as described by Faraday’s law. That principle underlies transformers and generators. The direction of the induced effect follows Lenz’s law: it opposes the change in flux that produced it. Electrical safety and appropriate current limits are essential in any practical experiment, even when the theory seems simple.

Worked examples

Question. A 12 V source is connected to a 6 Ω ideal resistor. Find current and power.

Solution, step by step. I = V/R = 12/6 = 2 A. Power = VI = 12 × 2 = 24 W. The resistor converts electrical energy to heat.

Worked example 2

Question. A 12 V battery is across a 4 Ω resistor. Find current and power.

Solution. Ohm’s law: I=V/R=12/4=3 A. Electrical power P=VI=12×3=36 W.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A 9 V source drives 0.3 A through a resistor. Find resistance.

Show worked answer

R = V/I = 9/0.3 = 30 Ω.

Problem 2 of 5

6 V applied to 2 Ω resistor: current?

Show answer and method

3 A.

Problem 3 of 5

Power at 5 V and 2 A?

Show answer and method

10 W.

Problem 4 of 5

Two 3 Ω resistors in series have total resistance?

Show answer and method

6 Ω.

Problem 5 of 5

How does magnetic force act on a moving charged particle relative to its velocity?

Show answer and method

Perpendicular to velocity for the magnetic part of the Lorentz force.

Try it yourself

Build a low-voltage circuit with a battery pack, suitable resistor and LED following component ratings. Compare series and parallel connections on paper.

Where this model stops

Do not connect a battery directly across a wire without current limiting; mains electricity is not appropriate for home experiments.
