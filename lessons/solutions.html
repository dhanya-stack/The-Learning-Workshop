Solutions and Concentrations

Why does adding more water make a drink less concentrated?

What happens when a substance dissolves?

Concentration tells us how much solute is present in a chosen amount of solution. Diluting with solvent changes the total volume but, if none is lost or reacts, conserves the amount of solute. This makes dilution calculations straightforward.

Dissolving a substance distributes its particles through a solvent, but not every mixture is a true solution. Salt can dissolve in water to form a homogeneous ionic solution; sand remains a suspension or sediment instead. The solvent and solute interact at molecular scale, and the extent of dissolution can depend strongly on temperature.

Concentration describes how much solute is present relative to a quantity of solution or solvent. Clear solutions can still be concentrated, and coloured solutions can be dilute. Observing colour alone is not a reliable quantitative measurement.

Concentration and dilution

Molarity c is moles of solute per litre of solution. In an idealised dilution, moles before and after are equal: c₁V₁ = c₂V₂. Mix thoroughly before assuming uniform concentration.

c = n/V; c₁V₁ = c₂V₂

Molarity c=n/V uses moles of solute per litre of final solution, not per litre of solvent added before mixing. If 0.10 mol of solute is present in 0.50 L of final solution, molarity is 0.20 mol/L. In a dilution where solute amount remains unchanged, c₁V₁=c₂V₂ can be used with consistent units.

Solubility imposes a limit on how much solute can remain dissolved under specified conditions. A saturated solution is at equilibrium with excess undissolved material, not necessarily one that contains an intuitively “large” amount. For quantitative work, temperature, units, chemical form and accurate final volumes matter.

Worked examples

Question. How much 2.0 mol/L stock solution is needed to prepare 100 mL of 0.50 mol/L solution?

Solution, step by step. c₁V₁ = c₂V₂. V₁ = (0.50 × 100)/2.0 = 25 mL. Add solvent until final solution volume reaches 100 mL.

Worked example 2

Question. Dissolve 0.2 mol solute in enough water for 0.5 L final solution. Find molarity.

Solution. c=n/V=0.2/0.5=0.4 mol/L.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A 1.0 mol/L solution is diluted from 50 mL to 200 mL. What is the final concentration?

Show worked answer

c₂ = 1.0 × 50/200 = 0.25 mol/L.

Problem 2 of 5

Concentration of 1 mol in 2 L solution?

Show answer and method

0.5 mol/L.

Problem 3 of 5

How many moles in 100 mL of 0.2 mol/L solution?

Show answer and method

0.1×0.2=0.02 mol.

Problem 4 of 5

Dilute 50 mL of 1 M stock to 250 mL. Final molarity?

Show answer and method

c₂=(1×50)/250=0.2 M.

Problem 5 of 5

Does dilution change the moles of solute when none is lost?

Show answer and method

No.

Try it yourself

Use coloured water and a measuring cup to see visually how adding solvent changes appearance; do not infer exact concentration from colour alone.

Where this model stops

Use the final solution volume, not simply the amount of solvent added. Not all volumes add exactly for real liquid mixtures.
