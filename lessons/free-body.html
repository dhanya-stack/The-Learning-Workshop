Forces, Free-Body Diagrams and Equilibrium

Why must we account for every force before predicting motion?

Isolating a body from its surroundings

A free-body diagram isolates one object and draws the external forces acting on it. It is a bookkeeping tool, not decoration. Once forces are separated into directions, Newton’s laws tell us whether the object is balanced or accelerating.

A free-body diagram is a deliberate choice to study one object at a time. Imagine a box sitting on a table. Gravity pulls the box downward, and the table exerts an upward normal force. The box pushes the table down too, but that last force belongs to the table's diagram, not the box's.

This separation is especially important for assemblies. A mounting screw, a bracket and a payload interact through contact forces, but each component can be isolated and analysed. Drawing all action–reaction partners on one diagram often makes every force seem to cancel and hides the actual loading of a part.

Drawing the forces and finding equilibrium

For an object at rest, the sum of forces in each direction is zero. For a moving object, net force equals mass times acceleration. The normal reaction acts perpendicular to a surface; friction, when present, acts tangentially.

ΣFₓ = maₓ; ΣFᵧ = maᵧ

Choose axes, identify external forces, split angled forces into components and write ΣFx=ma_x and ΣFy=ma_y. Static equilibrium means both translational acceleration and angular acceleration are zero, so moments also need to balance when rotation is possible. A resting box has vertical force balance, but a long shelf may require torque balance to find its support reactions.

If someone pushes horizontally on a 4 kg box with 12 N while friction acts backward with 4 N, the horizontal acceleration is (12−4)/4=2 m/s². The normal force remains mg on a horizontal surface if no other vertical forces act. A normal reaction is a contact force determined by constraints; it is not always equal to weight on a slope or during vertical acceleration.

Worked examples

Question. A 6 kg box rests on a level table, with no other vertical forces. Find the normal reaction. Use g = 9.81 m/s².

Solution, step by step. Its weight is mg = 6 × 9.81 = 58.86 N downward. Vertical acceleration is zero, so upward normal force N = 58.86 N.

Worked example 2

Question. A 5 kg crate rests on a level floor. Find the upward normal force (g=9.81 m/s²), assuming no other vertical forces.

Solution. Vertical equilibrium: N−mg=0. Therefore N=5×9.81=49.05 N.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A 2 kg block experiences 10 N east and 4 N west on a horizontal frictionless surface. Find acceleration.

Show worked answer

Net force = 10 − 4 = 6 N east. a = F/m = 6/2 = 3 m/s² east.

Problem 2 of 5

Two people pull equally in opposite directions with 100 N each. Net force?

Show answer and method

0 N.

Problem 3 of 5

A hanging 2 kg mass is stationary. Cable tension, g=10?

Show answer and method

20 N.

Problem 4 of 5

A free-body diagram of a sliding box should include velocity as a force arrow?

Show answer and method

No. Velocity is not a force.

Problem 5 of 5

What condition defines translational equilibrium?

Show answer and method

Vector sum of external forces is zero.

Try it yourself

Choose a book on a table and draw only forces acting on the book. Repeat while you gently push it sideways.

Where this model stops

Do not include forces the chosen object exerts on its surroundings in its own free-body diagram.
