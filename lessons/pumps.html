Pumps and Fluid Systems

How do you choose a pump for a real piping system?

How a pump moves fluid

A pump adds mechanical energy to a liquid. The piping system consumes that energy through elevation change and hydraulic losses. The operating flow is where the pump’s head–flow curve intersects the system head requirement.

A pump transfers mechanical energy from a driver to a fluid. It does not create water, and it does not necessarily impose one fixed flow rate regardless of the piping. The actual operating flow is found where the pump characteristic meets the demand of the connected system.

Imagine a pump circulating coolant through a heat exchanger. Opening a valve reduces system resistance and may increase flow; closing it increases resistance and usually reduces flow. The resulting pump head and efficiency change with the operating point. This is why selecting a pump from only one pressure and flow figure can be misleading.

Head, flow and pump performance

Pump head is energy per unit weight, usually expressed in metres of liquid. A simple model for a recirculating system is Hsystem = Hstatic + KQ²; actual losses require components and fluid properties. Hydraulic power is ρgQH.

P_hydraulic = ρgQH

Head expresses energy per unit weight of fluid, with metres of fluid as a common unit. A pump curve plots added head against flow rate at a specified speed and impeller. The system curve represents static requirements plus frictional losses, which commonly increase roughly with flow squared in turbulent regimes.

Power transferred to the fluid is P_h=ρgQH, and required shaft power is P_h/η when overall pump efficiency is η. For water flowing at 0.002 m³/s against 15 m head, hydraulic power is about 294 W; with 60% efficiency, shaft demand is about 490 W. Check net positive suction head, operating range and cavitation risk as part of pump selection, not as an afterthought.

Worked examples

Question. A water pump delivers 0.010 m³/s against a total head of 15 m. Estimate hydraulic output power.

Solution, step by step. P = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.010 × 15 = 1471.5 W ≈ 1.47 kW. With a 70% overall efficiency, electrical input would be about 1.47/0.70 ≈ 2.10 kW.

Worked example 2

Question. A pump raises water at 0.01 m³/s through 20 m head. Estimate hydraulic power for ρ=1000 kg/m³, g=9.81.

Solution. P=ρgQH=1000×9.81×0.01×20=1962 W≈1.96 kW; shaft power is higher if efficiency is <100%.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. If a pump delivers 0.005 m³/s at 10 m head, find the hydraulic power for water.

Show worked answer

P = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.005 × 10 = 490.5 W.

Problem 2 of 5

Compute hydraulic power at Q=0.005 m³/s and H=10 m for water, g=10.

Show answer and method

500 W.

Problem 3 of 5

If pump efficiency is 50% and hydraulic power is 1 kW, shaft input?

Show answer and method

2 kW.

Problem 4 of 5

What sets the operating point of a pump and piping system?

Show answer and method

Intersection of pump head curve and system head curve.

Problem 5 of 5

Why does raising a tank vertically increase static head?

Show answer and method

The required gravitational potential energy per unit weight increases.

Try it yourself

Sketch an imagined falling pump head curve and rising system curve. Move the valve position in your sketch and reason about the new operating point.

Where this model stops

A pump’s nameplate maximum flow and maximum head usually do not occur at the same operating point.
