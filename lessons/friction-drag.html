Friction and Drag

Why is it harder to start sliding a box than to keep it moving?

Why motion is resisted

Friction is a contact force opposing relative sliding or its tendency. Static friction adjusts up to a maximum value; kinetic friction acts when surfaces slide. Drag is resistance from a surrounding fluid and often varies with speed.

When two solid surfaces slide against one another, microscopic contact interactions resist relative motion. Static friction can adjust to match the applied tangential force up to a limiting value; kinetic friction applies during sliding and is often modelled approximately with a constant coefficient. Neither coefficient tells the whole story about real materials.

Fluid drag is different. Air and water flow around a moving body, producing pressure and viscous forces that typically oppose its motion relative to the fluid. Cyclists feel drag rise with speed; engineers try to reduce it through smoother shapes, while parachutes deliberately increase it.

Frictional forces and fluid drag

In a simple dry-friction model, maximum static friction equals μₛN and kinetic friction roughly equals μₖN. A common high-speed drag approximation uses density, speed squared, projected area and a drag coefficient.

Fₛ,max = μₛN; Fₖ ≈ μₖN; D ≈ ½ρCᴅAv²

A common dry-friction model uses |F_s|≤μ_sN for static contact and F_k≈μ_kN during sliding. For a crate on a horizontal floor, N≈mg when there are no other vertical loads. The static inequality matters: friction is not automatically μ_sN before slipping begins.

For many objects moving fast enough through air, drag can be approximated by F_D=½ρC_DAv², where ρ is fluid density, A a reference area and C_D a shape-dependent coefficient. The quadratic law is not universal; very slow viscous flows can behave differently. If speed doubles while other factors remain the same in the quadratic regime, drag increases fourfold. Aerodynamic coefficient values depend on geometry and flow conditions and often need testing.

Worked examples

Question. A 10 kg crate has μₛ = 0.40 on a horizontal floor. Estimate the smallest horizontal push needed to start sliding.

Solution, step by step. Normal force N = mg = 10 × 9.81 = 98.1 N. Maximum static friction = 0.40 × 98.1 = 39.24 N. The applied push must just exceed about 39.2 N.

Worked example 2

Question. A 10 kg box rests on horizontal floor with μk=0.3; find kinetic friction while sliding (g=10 m/s²).

Solution. Normal force N=mg=100 N. Kinetic friction =μkN=0.3×100=30 N opposite relative motion.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A 5 kg block has μₖ = 0.20 on a level floor. Approximate kinetic friction.

Show worked answer

Fₖ = μₖmg = 0.20 × 5 × 9.81 ≈ 9.81 N.

Problem 2 of 5

If μk=0.2 and N=50 N, kinetic friction?

Show answer and method

10 N.

Problem 3 of 5

Why does air drag usually increase with speed?

Show answer and method

Faster relative flow generally increases momentum transfer and resistive force.

Problem 4 of 5

Can static friction be zero?

Show answer and method

Yes, if no force tends to cause slip.

Problem 5 of 5

Does the basic law Fk=μkN give the direction of friction by itself?

Show answer and method

No; friction opposes relative sliding direction.

Try it yourself

Slide a small object over paper, fabric and a smooth desk. Predict which surface creates more resistance before testing.

Where this model stops

Friction coefficients vary with material, loading and conditions; quadratic drag is not appropriate for every flow regime.
