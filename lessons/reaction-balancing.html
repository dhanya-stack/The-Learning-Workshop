Chemical Reactions and Balancing Equations

Why must atoms balance even when the substances look completely different?

What stays the same in a chemical reaction?

Chemical reactions rearrange atoms into new combinations. In an ordinary chemical reaction, each element’s atoms are conserved. A balanced equation counts those atoms on both sides; coefficients change amounts, not the identity of substances.

A chemical equation describes reactants becoming products, but atoms are rearranged rather than created or destroyed in ordinary chemical reactions. Balancing an equation is therefore more than a bookkeeping exercise; it expresses conservation of each element. Coefficients tell us relative amounts of substances, not how fast a reaction proceeds.

For example, burning hydrogen in oxygen creates water. There are two oxygen atoms in an O₂ molecule, so a balanced equation must account for both. Changing H₂O into H₂O₂ merely to fix the count would describe a different substance and is not an acceptable balancing step.

Atoms, coefficients and balanced equations

To balance, list atom counts for each element, adjust whole-number coefficients, and recheck. Never change chemical subscripts to make the bookkeeping work: that would change the chemical species.

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

The balanced equation is 2H₂+O₂→2H₂O. It says that two moles of hydrogen molecules react with one mole of oxygen molecules to produce two moles of water, under the ideal stoichiometric interpretation. Subscripts stay fixed because they define chemical identity; coefficients can change to balance amounts.

Some reactions require additional constraints. For ionic equations, both atoms and net electric charge must balance. A balanced equation does not demonstrate that the proposed reaction is chemically feasible, safe or complete. It provides the quantitative ratios used later in stoichiometry once the actual chemistry is established.

Worked examples

Question. Balance the formation of water from H₂ and O₂.

Solution, step by step. Start with H₂ + O₂ → H₂O. Put 2 before H₂O to balance oxygen, giving 4 H atoms on the right. Put 2 before H₂ to balance hydrogen: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

Worked example 2

Question. Balance hydrogen combustion H₂ + O₂ → H₂O.

Solution. Choose 2 H₂O so O atoms are 2 on the right. Then 2 H₂ balances 4 H atoms. Balanced: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. Balance Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃ using the smallest whole numbers.

Show worked answer

4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃. Both sides have 4 Fe atoms and 6 O atoms.

Problem 2 of 5

Balance Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃.

Show answer and method

4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃.

Problem 3 of 5

Balance N₂ + H₂ → NH₃.

Show answer and method

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃.

Problem 4 of 5

Can coefficients change but subscripts stay fixed when balancing?

Show answer and method

Yes: change only coefficients to conserve atom counts.

Problem 5 of 5

What physical principle motivates balancing equations?

Show answer and method

Conservation of atoms of each element in ordinary chemical reactions.

Try it yourself

Use coloured paper circles to represent atoms before and after a symbolic reaction; ensure no circles vanish.

Where this model stops

A balanced equation describes conservation, not necessarily how rapidly the reaction happens or its mechanism.
