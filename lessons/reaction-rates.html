Reaction Rates and Catalysts

Why can a reaction be favourable yet proceed almost imperceptibly slowly?

Why some reactions happen faster

Thermodynamics concerns energy and equilibrium, while kinetics concerns how fast change occurs. Reaction rate depends on collision opportunities and reaction pathways. Higher temperature often increases the fraction of collisions energetic enough to react.

Two reactions with the same overall energy change can proceed at completely different speeds. Iron corrodes slowly in many ordinary environments, while some combustion reactions proceed extremely rapidly after ignition. Reaction rate depends on pathways and activation barriers, not simply on whether the reaction releases energy.

Concentration, temperature, available surface area and catalysts can influence how frequently reactive encounters occur and how likely they are to produce products. Crushing a soluble solid may speed its dissolution by increasing exposed surface area, but dissolution itself should not be confused with a chemical reaction.

Collisions, temperature and catalysts

A basic rate law may be rate = k[A]^m, but the exponent m must generally come from measurements, not guesses from the balanced equation. Catalysts provide an alternative pathway with lower activation barrier.

rate = k[A]^m (empirical rate law)

Rate laws are measured relationships such as rate=k[A]^m[B]^n. The exponents are reaction orders determined experimentally; they cannot normally be read directly from overall balanced-equation coefficients. Temperature affects the rate constant, often approximated by the Arrhenius relationship k=Aexp(−Ea/RT).

A catalyst offers a different reaction pathway with a lower effective energy barrier and is regenerated through the catalytic cycle. It does not change the equilibrium constant at the same temperature. Real reactions may have several elementary steps and intermediates, so a simple one-step collision picture may explain trends but not full mechanisms.

Worked examples

Question. For a first-order reaction with rate = k[A], k = 0.20 s⁻¹ and [A] = 0.50 mol/L, find the instantaneous rate.

Solution, step by step. rate = 0.20 × 0.50 = 0.10 mol/(L·s). This value applies at the stated concentration.

Worked example 2

Question. A concentration falls from 0.80 to 0.50 mol/L in 10 s. Find average disappearance rate.

Solution. Rate of disappearance = −Δc/Δt = (0.80−0.50)/10 = 0.030 mol/(L·s).

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. For the same law, what is the rate when [A] falls to 0.25 mol/L?

Show worked answer

rate = 0.20 × 0.25 = 0.050 mol/(L·s). Halving [A] halves rate in this first-order model.

Problem 2 of 5

What normally happens to reaction rate when temperature rises?

Show answer and method

Usually increases; more particles cross activation-energy barriers.

Problem 3 of 5

What does a catalyst lower?

Show answer and method

Activation energy along an alternative reaction pathway.

Problem 4 of 5

If concentration changes by 0.2 mol/L over 4 s, average magnitude of rate?

Show answer and method

0.05 mol/(L·s).

Problem 5 of 5

Why might powder react faster than a solid block of equal mass?

Show answer and method

Powder exposes greater surface area for heterogeneous reaction.

Try it yourself

Observe how an effervescent tablet behaves in cool versus moderately warm water using a safe, unsealed container.

Where this model stops

A catalyst changes rate, not the position of equilibrium at fixed temperature, and the empirical order need not equal a reaction coefficient.
