Chemical Equilibrium

How can a reaction keep going while its amounts stop changing?

Why reactions do not always go to completion

At dynamic chemical equilibrium, forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates. Concentrations remain stable, but molecules are still reacting. Changing concentrations, pressure or temperature can shift the equilibrium position.

Many chemical reactions proceed in both directions. At equilibrium, forward and reverse reaction rates are equal, so macroscopic composition stops changing even though molecules continue reacting. Equilibrium is dynamic, not a sign that all chemical activity has ceased.

A closed bottle of carbonated drink offers an everyday connection. Dissolved carbon dioxide and gas in the space above the liquid can reach an equilibrium. Opening the bottle changes the pressure and conditions, and bubbles form as the system moves toward a new state.

The balance between forward and reverse reactions

For a simple reversible reaction A ⇌ B, the equilibrium constant Kc describes the ratio of equilibrium concentrations according to the balanced reaction. A large K favours products, but does not indicate how quickly equilibrium is reached.

For A ⇌ B: Kc = [B]/[A]

For a reaction aA+bB⇌cC+dD, an equilibrium constant is formed from activities of products and reactants raised to their stoichiometric coefficients. Under suitable approximations, concentrations may be used in a familiar Kc expression. The numerical value depends on temperature and indicates the relative equilibrium composition.

Changing concentration or pressure can shift the equilibrium composition, but a catalyst changes forward and reverse reaction rates without changing K or the equilibrium state at fixed temperature. It helps reach equilibrium faster. Le Châtelier’s principle is a useful qualitative guide, though quantitative predictions require the actual equilibrium relationships and constraints.

Worked examples

Question. At equilibrium for A ⇌ B, [A] = 0.20 mol/L and [B] = 0.60 mol/L. Find Kc.

Solution, step by step. Kc = 0.60/0.20 = 3, dimensionless for this simplified reaction. The equilibrium mixture contains more B than A.

Worked example 2

Question. For N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g), predict effect of decreasing volume at fixed temperature.

Solution. Pressure rises; the equilibrium tends to shift toward fewer gas moles: N₂O₄ side. This is a qualitative Le Châtelier prediction.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. If [A] = 0.50 mol/L and [B] = 0.25 mol/L for A ⇌ B, calculate Kc.

Show worked answer

Kc = 0.25/0.50 = 0.5.

Problem 2 of 5

At equilibrium, must reactant and product concentrations be equal?

Show answer and method

No: forward and reverse rates are equal.

Problem 3 of 5

Adding a reactant often shifts equilibrium which way?

Show answer and method

Toward products, if other conditions remain suitable.

Problem 4 of 5

Does a catalyst change equilibrium composition?

Show answer and method

No; it speeds approach to equilibrium.

Problem 5 of 5

For an exothermic forward reaction, increasing temperature favours which direction?

Show answer and method

The reverse (endothermic) direction.

Try it yourself

Model A and B as beads in two cups. Move beads both directions each round while adjusting rates until net counts stabilise.

Where this model stops

Equilibrium constants depend on temperature; adding a catalyst changes how quickly equilibrium is reached, not K at fixed temperature.
