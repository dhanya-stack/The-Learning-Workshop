Dot Products and Cross Products

What makes one kind of vector multiplication describe work and another turning?

Two ways to combine vectors

The dot product measures how aligned two vectors are; the cross product measures their perpendicular interaction and has a direction. Both use the same pair of vectors yet answer different physical questions.

Two vector products answer different geometric questions. The dot product asks how much one vector points along another. The cross product asks how strongly they point across one another and gives a direction perpendicular to both. That difference becomes crucial when calculating work and torque.

Pushing a crate horizontally performs mechanical work through the horizontal part of your force. If you also pull upward, that vertical component makes no contribution to the work done during a purely horizontal displacement. By contrast, when tightening a bolt, the turning effect depends on how nearly perpendicular your push is to the wrench.

Projection, torque and direction

A force acting along a displacement does work according to its dot product. A force at a lever arm produces torque according to a cross product; only the perpendicular part contributes. In 2D, the torque direction is perpendicular to the plane.

A · B = |A||B| cos θ; |A × B| = |A||B| sin θ

The dot product a·b=|a||b|cosθ is a scalar. It is zero for perpendicular vectors and positive or negative according to their relative directions. The cross product magnitude |a×b|=|a||b|sinθ is greatest for perpendicular vectors and zero for parallel ones; its direction follows the right-hand rule.

For example, a 20 N force applied perpendicular to a 0.3 m lever arm generates a torque magnitude of 6 N·m. The same force applied along the lever arm produces zero torque about the pivot. In Cartesian coordinates, the dot product is axbx+ayby+azbz; the cross product uses component combinations and produces another vector. Neither operation can generally be substituted for the other.

Worked examples

Question. A 20 N force acts 60° from a 3 m displacement. What work is done?

Solution, step by step. Work = Fd cos θ = 20 × 3 × cos 60° = 30 J. Only the component of force parallel to movement transfers energy in this model.

Worked example 2

Question. Calculate a·b for a=(2,−1,3) and b=(4,0,2).

Solution. Multiply matching components and sum: 2×4 + (−1)×0 + 3×2 = 14.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A 5 N force is perpendicular to a 0.4 m lever. Find the torque magnitude.

Show worked answer

τ = rF sin 90° = 0.4 × 5 = 2 N·m.

Problem 2 of 5

Find (3,4)·(0,2).

Show answer and method

3×0 + 4×2 = 8.

Problem 3 of 5

What is the dot product of perpendicular nonzero vectors?

Show answer and method

Zero.

Problem 4 of 5

For a=(1,0,0), b=(0,1,0), find a×b.

Show answer and method

(0,0,1), by the right-hand rule.

Problem 5 of 5

If two vectors point in the same direction, what is the sign of their dot product?

Show answer and method

Positive, provided neither vector is zero.

Try it yourself

Hold a ruler by one end and press it at different angles. Which push turns it most effectively? Relate your observation to the sine factor.

Where this model stops

The dot product is a scalar; the cross product is a vector. In 3D its direction follows the right-hand rule.
