Atomic structure

Meet protons, neutrons and electrons; discover what makes each element—and isotope—different.

Matter is built from atoms

Imagine cutting a sample into smaller and smaller pieces. An atom is the smallest unit that retains the chemical identity of an element. Its tiny nucleus contains positively charged protons and (usually) neutrons; negatively charged electrons occupy regions around it.

The number of protons—the atomic number Z—determines the element. Changing the neutron count makes a different isotope; changing the electron count gives an ion.

Mass number A = protons + neutrons

For a neutral atom, electron count equals proton count. The mass number is an integer count of nucleons, not the average atomic mass shown on a periodic table.

Matter is made of atoms, but an atom is not a miniature solar system with electrons following fixed little planetary paths. It consists of a small dense nucleus containing protons and usually neutrons, surrounded by electrons whose behaviour is described by quantum mechanics. The positive nuclear charge attracts the negatively charged electrons.

The number of protons identifies the element: six protons makes carbon, regardless of its neutron count. Neutrons can vary within one element, giving isotopes; electrons can be gained or lost to form ions. These distinctions explain why atomic identity, atomic mass and electrical charge are different properties.

Why the usual atom picture is only a model

A common illustration shows electrons as dots on concentric circles. That is useful for learning shell occupancy, but real electrons do not orbit like tiny planets; quantum mechanics describes probability distributions called orbitals.

We'll use a simplified shell model here for the first ten elements. It is a teaching picture, not a true scale drawing of electron clouds or the nucleus.

An atom's atomic number Z counts protons, while the mass number A counts protons plus neutrons. A neutral atom has Z electrons; an ion may have more or fewer electrons. Carbon-12 contains six protons and six neutrons, while carbon-14 contains six protons and eight neutrons. They have very similar chemical identity but different nuclear properties.

The electrons occupy allowed energy states rather than arbitrary energies. When an atom absorbs or emits a photon with the right energy, it can move between states. This helps explain characteristic spectra. Diagrams showing circular electron orbits can introduce the nucleus and shells, but should be treated as simplified teaching models, not literal pictures of electron trajectories.

Worked examples

Problem: A carbon-12 atom has atomic number 6 and mass number 12. How many protons, neutrons and electrons does it have?
  1. Atomic number = protons = 6.
  2. Neutrons = mass number − protons = 12 − 6 = 6.
  3. A neutral atom has the same number of electrons as protons: 6 electrons.

The 12 identifies a particular isotope. Carbon-13 would have 7 neutrons.

Worked example 2

Question. An aluminium-27 atom has atomic number 13. Count protons, neutrons, electrons in a neutral atom.

Solution. Protons = atomic number = 13. Neutrons = mass number − protons = 27−13=14. Neutral electrons = 13.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5
Your turn: A sodium-23 ion has charge +1 and atomic number 11. Find its protons, neutrons and electrons.
Show worked answer

Protons = 11; neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12. A +1 ion has lost one electron, so electrons = 10.

Problem 2 of 5

Carbon-14 has Z=6: neutrons?

Show answer and method

14−6=8.

Problem 3 of 5

A neutral atom contains 8 protons. Electrons?

Show answer and method

8.

Problem 4 of 5

An ion has 12 protons and 10 electrons. Charge?

Show answer and method

2+.

Problem 5 of 5

Do isotopes of one element have different proton counts?

Show answer and method

No; they differ in neutron number.

Try a periodic-table investigation

Find hydrogen, carbon and oxygen on a periodic table. Compare atomic numbers and ask: what exactly changes when you move from one element to the next?

Extension: Sketch carbon-12 and carbon-13. What stays the same—and why do both remain carbon?
