Energy in Chemical Reactions

Why can some reactions warm a container while others cool it?

Why reactions release or absorb heat

Chemical reactions involve breaking and forming bonds, changing the energy of the system and surroundings. Exothermic processes release heat under a specified condition; endothermic processes absorb it. A reaction’s enthalpy change compares products with reactants.

Chemical reactions can warm their surroundings or cool them because reactants and products have different energy states. Some processes release energy to the surroundings (exothermic), while others absorb energy (endothermic). This says something about heat exchange at specified conditions, not whether the reaction happens immediately or is safe.

A burning fuel can release substantial heat but still require ignition because an activation-energy barrier must be overcome. Conversely, a process can be energetically favourable yet proceed extremely slowly. Separating thermodynamics from reaction kinetics helps resolve these apparent contradictions.

Enthalpy and energy accounting

For a reaction at approximately constant pressure, heat transfer is often described by ΔH. A negative ΔH is exothermic by the usual convention. A simple calorimetry estimate uses q = mcΔT for a measured surrounding liquid.

q = mcΔT; ΔHreaction ≈ −qsurroundings at constant pressure

At constant pressure, reaction enthalpy ΔH describes the heat exchanged for a reaction as written, under appropriate conditions. A negative ΔH indicates an exothermic change with the usual sign convention. If an exothermic reaction releases 100 kJ per mole of reaction and proceeds to 0.2 mol of reaction extent, its ideal heat release is 20 kJ.

Hess's law states that enthalpy change depends on initial and final states, allowing reaction steps to be added algebraically. Bond-energy estimates are rough because average bond energies may not represent specific molecular environments. A calorimetry calculation also needs to account for heat capacity and any heat lost to the apparatus or surroundings.

Worked examples

Question. A reaction warms 100 g of water from 20°C to 25°C. Estimate heat gained by water using c = 4.18 J/(g·K).

Solution, step by step. qwater = 100 × 4.18 × 5 = 2090 J = 2.09 kJ. If other heat transfers are negligible, the reaction released about 2.09 kJ.

Worked example 2

Question. A reaction releases 25 kJ per mole. What heat is released by 0.4 mol at the stated conditions?

Solution. 0.4×25=10 kJ released; reaction enthalpy is −10 kJ for that amount.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. If 50 g of water rises by 3°C, estimate its heat gain with c = 4.18 J/(g·K).

Show worked answer

q = 50 × 4.18 × 3 = 627 J.

Problem 2 of 5

Is a reaction that releases heat exothermic or endothermic?

Show answer and method

Exothermic.

Problem 3 of 5

If ΔH=+40 kJ for a reaction as written, does it absorb or release heat?

Show answer and method

Absorb 40 kJ at constant pressure.

Problem 4 of 5

What units are often used for molar enthalpy change?

Show answer and method

kJ/mol.

Problem 5 of 5

Does a catalyst change the overall enthalpy change of a reaction?

Show answer and method

No; it changes pathway and activation energy.

Try it yourself

Compare temperature change when safe substances dissolve in water, using only materials and procedures approved for home use.

Where this model stops

Temperature change alone does not determine reaction enthalpy without masses, heat capacity and an estimate of heat losses.
