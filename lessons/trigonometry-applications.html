Trigonometry and Its Applications

Can you estimate the height of a building without going inside?

Finding distances without measuring them directly

Sine, cosine and tangent connect angles with length ratios in right triangles. Their value is practical: if one length and an angle are available, you can infer an inaccessible dimension.

Trigonometry is useful whenever a distance or height is difficult to measure directly. Imagine standing a known horizontal distance from a building and measuring the angle to its roof. A right triangle appears even though there is no drawn triangle on the building. Geometry turns the angle into a height estimate.

Sine and cosine also describe how rotating quantities project onto horizontal and vertical axes. That makes them useful for ramps, cranes, robotics and cyclical motion. The familiar SOH–CAH–TOA rules are a starting point, not the whole subject; the deeper idea is that the ratios depend on angle rather than the triangle's overall size.

Triangles, angles and sine/cosine

For an elevation angle θ, tangent is opposite divided by adjacent. If you stand horizontal distance d from a wall and measure the angle to the top, the height above your eye level is d tan θ. Add the height of the observer’s eye for total building height.

tan θ = opposite/adjacent; height ≈ d tan θ + eye height

For a right triangle, tanθ=opposite/adjacent. If you measure a roof at 30° above the horizontal from 20 m away, the estimated height above your eye level is 20tan30° ≈11.55 m. You must still add your eye height to obtain the total building height and check that the ground is approximately level.

Real measurements introduce uncertainty. If the angle shifts by even a small amount, the estimated height changes—especially for steep sight lines. Try calculating the height using θ=29° and θ=31° to see the sensitivity. For non-right triangles, sine and cosine rules extend the idea. For periodic processes, sine and cosine connect angular motion with repeating time signals.

Worked examples

Question. A student stands 20 m from a tower. The angle to its top is 35° and eye level is 1.6 m. Estimate the tower height.

Solution, step by step. Height above the eyes = 20 tan 35° ≈ 14.00 m. Total ≈ 14.00 + 1.60 = 15.60 m.

Worked example 2

Question. A ladder 5 m long leans at 60° above horizontal. How high does it reach?

Solution. Vertical height = 5 sin60° = (5√3)/2 ≈ 4.33 m.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. With horizontal distance 12 m, elevation angle 45° and eye height 1.5 m, find the height.

Show worked answer

12 tan 45° + 1.5 = 12 + 1.5 = 13.5 m.

Problem 2 of 5

Find sin30° and cos60°.

Show answer and method

Both equal 0.5.

Problem 3 of 5

A right triangle has opposite=3 and adjacent=4. Find tanθ.

Show answer and method

3/4 = 0.75.

Problem 4 of 5

A 10 m slope makes 30° with horizontal. What is its horizontal projection?

Show answer and method

10 cos30° ≈ 8.66 m.

Problem 5 of 5

Convert 180° to radians.

Show answer and method

π radians.

Try it yourself

Use a paper protractor, string and weighted washer to make a simple clinometer. Try measuring a tree with a known horizontal distance.
