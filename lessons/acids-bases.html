Acids and bases

Understand the logarithmic pH scale, compare solutions, and try a simple colour-indicator experiment.

Acids and bases in everyday life

Lemon juice, vinegar and some cleaning solutions are associated with acidity or basicity. A useful way of describing an aqueous solution is its pH.

At about 25°C, neutral pure water has pH 7. A lower pH means a higher hydrogen-ion activity; a higher pH indicates a lower hydrogen-ion activity. pH is logarithmic: a difference of one unit corresponds to a factor of ten in hydrogen-ion activity.

For dilute ideal solutions: pH ≈ −log₁₀[H⁺]

The concentration [H⁺] is expressed in mol/L relative to the standard state. This approximation is useful for introductory problems, though precise pH is defined using activity.

An acid–base reaction often involves transfer of a proton (H⁺) between chemical species. Everyday examples include vinegar, lemon juice and baking soda solutions, but taste is not a safe method of identifying chemicals. Acidity and basicity describe chemical behaviour rather than simply whether a liquid feels strong or weak.

Water can act as an acid or a base depending on its reaction partner. An aqueous solution contains a balance of hydronium and hydroxide ions; changing that balance alters pH. An indicator changes colour because its molecular forms respond differently to acidity—not because it mechanically “measures” the amount of acid present.

What a colour indicator tells us

Some plant pigments change colour depending on solution acidity. Red-cabbage indicator is a familiar example. An indicator provides a rough classification; exact pH requires suitable calibration or a pH meter.

For dilute aqueous solutions, pH=−log₁₀(a_H⁺), commonly approximated as −log₁₀[H⁺] when activity effects are negligible. A tenfold change in hydrogen-ion activity changes pH by one unit. A strong acid dissociates extensively in water, whereas a weak acid only partially dissociates; “strong” and “concentrated” are distinct ideas.

Neutralisation transfers protons and often yields water and a salt, but the pH at the equivalence point is not always 7, especially for weak acid–strong base or weak base–strong acid combinations. For home experiments, indicator solution made from red cabbage can show qualitative changes using dilute kitchen materials. Avoid mixing household cleaners or attempting to identify unknown chemicals by smell.

Worked examples

Problem: Solution A has pH 3 and solution B has pH 5. In the dilute approximation, how do their hydrogen-ion concentrations compare?
  1. Difference in pH = 5 − 3 = 2.
  2. Each pH step is a factor of 10 in hydrogen-ion concentration.
  3. 10² = 100. Solution A has approximately 100 times the hydrogen-ion concentration of solution B.

This compares hydrogen-ion concentration, not directly the overall corrosiveness or safety of either solution.

Worked example 2

Question. A dilute strong-acid solution has [H⁺]=1×10⁻³ mol/L. Estimate pH at standard dilute-solution approximation.

Solution. pH=−log₁₀[H⁺]=−log₁₀(10⁻³)=3. This is approximate because activity effects are neglected.

Practice set

Problem 1 of 5
Your turn: A dilute strong-acid solution has [H⁺] = 1 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L. Estimate its pH.
Show worked answer

pH = −log₁₀(10⁻⁴) = 4.

Problem 2 of 5

Estimate pH when [H⁺]=10⁻⁵ mol/L in dilute solution.

Show answer and method

5.

Problem 3 of 5

How many times more concentrated is [H⁺] at pH 2 than pH 4?

Show answer and method

100 times.

Problem 4 of 5

Is pH 3 typically acidic or basic in water?

Show answer and method

Acidic.

Problem 5 of 5

What colour change might red-cabbage indicator show between acidic and basic solutions?

Show answer and method

Typically reddish/pink in acid and greenish/yellow toward strongly basic, depending on concentration.

Try a safe kitchen indicator

With supervision where appropriate, soak chopped red cabbage in warm water, let it cool, and strain. Place small samples in separate cups and add a little lemon juice to one and baking-soda solution to another. Observe how their colours differ.

Safety: Wear eye protection, do not taste test solutions, and never mix cleaning products or use bleach in this experiment. Dispose of samples after the activity.
