Vector Components and Resultants

Why is it easier to calculate horizontal and vertical forces separately?

Breaking one vector into two directions

A sloping force is one arrow but can be represented as perpendicular components. The components are not extra forces: they are two contributions which together equal the original force. The choice of axes is part of the modelling.

A slanted force often affects a machine in more than one direction. Rather than calculating separately with awkward angles each time, resolve the force into perpendicular components. The two components together reproduce the original vector; neither component is an additional force being created.

Suppose you pull a suitcase with a handle tilted upward. Part of your pull moves the suitcase forward and part lifts on it, changing the normal force and therefore friction. Resolving that one pull into horizontal and vertical directions reveals both effects. The choice of coordinate axes is up to you, so choose axes that simplify the physical situation.

Projections and resultant vectors

If a force F makes angle θ with the positive horizontal axis, its horizontal component is F cos θ, and its vertical component is F sin θ. Resultants come from adding corresponding components, then reconstructing the size and direction.

Fₓ = F cos θ; Fᵧ = F sin θ; |R| = √(Rₓ² + Rᵧ²)

For a vector of magnitude F at angle θ measured from the positive horizontal axis, the components are Fx=F cosθ and Fy=F sinθ. They carry signs: a force pointing left has a negative x-component. Conversely, the magnitude of perpendicular components is sqrt(Fx²+Fy²), and direction is found from the component signs and an appropriate inverse tangent.

A resultant is found by adding x-components together and y-components together before calculating the final magnitude. This is much safer than adding vector magnitudes. In three dimensions we add a z-component, and the same bookkeeping works for loads on fasteners, motion in space, and the combined influence of several thrusters. The coordinate system changes the numbers assigned to the components, but not the physical vector.

Worked examples

Question. Resolve a 10 N pull acting at 30° above horizontal.

Solution, step by step. Horizontal: 10 cos 30° ≈ 8.66 N. Vertical: 10 sin 30° = 5.00 N. Check: √(8.66² + 5²) ≈ 10 N.

Worked example 2

Question. Resolve a 20 N force at 60° above the horizontal.

Solution. Fx = 20 cos60° = 10 N. Fy = 20 sin60° ≈ 17.32 N.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A force has components 3 N east and 4 N north. Find its magnitude and angle above east.

Show worked answer

Magnitude = √(3² + 4²) = 5 N. Angle = tan⁻¹(4/3) ≈ 53.1°.

Problem 2 of 5

Resolve 10 N horizontally right into x and y components.

Show answer and method

Fx = 10 N, Fy = 0.

Problem 3 of 5

Combine forces (4, 3) and (−1, 2) N.

Show answer and method

Resultant = (3, 5) N.

Problem 4 of 5

A force has components −6 N in x and 8 N in y. Find magnitude.

Show answer and method

√(36+64) = 10 N.

Problem 5 of 5

Why must the angle reference axis be stated?

Show answer and method

Because sine/cosine component assignments depend on how the angle is defined.

Try it yourself

Sketch several arrows head to tail on graph paper. Add their x- and y-components and compare with the final drawn arrow.

Where this model stops

Sine and cosine swap roles if you measure the angle from a different axis. First mark the angle on a sketch.
