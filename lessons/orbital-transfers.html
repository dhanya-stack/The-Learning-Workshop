Orbital Transfers and Manoeuvres

Why can a spacecraft speed up to enter a higher orbit but arrive moving more slowly?

Changing orbits takes energy

Changing orbits is about changing energy and angular momentum, not simply choosing a new altitude. In a Hohmann transfer between coplanar circular orbits, two ideal impulses move the craft onto an ellipse and then circularise it.

Moving a spacecraft to a new orbit is not just changing its altitude. A brief thruster burn changes velocity, which changes the shape and orientation of the subsequent trajectory. A spacecraft may temporarily occupy an elliptical transfer orbit before reaching a new circular orbit.

Counterintuitively, raising a circular orbit eventually produces a lower orbital speed. To get there, the spacecraft first accelerates at the lower orbit to reach a trajectory with a higher farthest point, then accelerates again at that farthest point to circularise. Energy and angular momentum explain why these steps fit together.

Delta-v and transfer manoeuvres

The vis-viva equation gives orbital speed at radius r on an orbit with semi-major axis a. On a higher circular orbit, the steady orbital speed is lower, even though reaching that orbit required positive Δv along the transfer.

v = √(μ(2/r − 1/a))

A Hohmann transfer between two coplanar circular orbits uses two impulsive burns connected by a half-ellipse. The transfer ellipse has semi-major axis a_t=(r₁+r₂)/2. The vis-viva equation v²=μ(2/r−1/a) gives speed at each point of a Keplerian orbit; comparing these speeds with circular-orbit speeds gives the ideal Δv values.

These calculations assume a two-body system and instantaneous burns. Plane changes can be expensive, finite burns alter the trajectory, and low-thrust electric propulsion follows a different path. Mission planning must also consider timing, eclipse, communications windows and collision avoidance.

Worked examples

Question. Earth’s μ ≈ 3.986×10¹⁴ m³/s². Estimate circular speed at radius r = 7.0×10⁶ m.

Solution, step by step. For a circle a = r, so v = √(μ/r) = √(3.986×10¹⁴ / 7.0×10⁶) ≈ 7546 m/s = 7.55 km/s.

Worked example 2

Question. A manoeuvre changes spacecraft speed from 7.50 to 7.62 km/s impulsively, with the thrust direction aligned to velocity. Find |Δv|.

Solution. Δv magnitude =7.62−7.50=0.12 km/s=120 m/s. This is not the total Δv of a full transfer.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. At the same central body, if a circular orbital radius quadruples, what happens to circular speed?

Show worked answer

v ∝ 1/√r, so speed is halved.

Problem 2 of 5

Convert 0.35 km/s Δv to m/s.

Show answer and method

350 m/s.

Problem 3 of 5

What is a Hohmann transfer?

Show answer and method

An ideal two-impulse coplanar transfer using a tangent ellipse between circular orbits.

Problem 4 of 5

At the lower orbit of a Hohmann transfer to a higher circle, first burn prograde or retrograde?

Show answer and method

Prograde.

Problem 5 of 5

Why do plane changes usually cost Δv?

Show answer and method

Changing velocity direction requires vector velocity change.

Try it yourself

Draw two concentric circular orbits and an ellipse tangent to each. Mark where the two ideal transfer burns occur.

Where this model stops

A real transfer may require plane changes, finite-duration thrust and navigation corrections; the two-burn sketch is a starting model.
