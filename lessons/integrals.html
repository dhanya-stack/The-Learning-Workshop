Integrals: adding up change

What happens when we add up many tiny changes? Explore the area under a graph and recover a total from a rate.

One number from many small pieces

Suppose a cyclist’s velocity increases over time. The speed at any instant tells us how fast they are moving, but how far have they travelled during a few seconds? We can split the time into small slices: velocity × duration is approximately the distance in each slice.

Add all the slices, make them thinner and thinner, and the estimate approaches a definite integral. On a velocity–time graph, it is the signed area between the curve and the time axis. For positive velocity throughout our example, this area is also distance travelled.

Imagine filling a tank at a changing rate. Knowing the instantaneous inflow in litres per second tells you what is happening now, not how much water has entered altogether. The integral answers the accumulation question by adding many small contributions over time. Each contribution can be approximated by flow rate multiplied by a short time interval.

This is closely related to finding area under a curve, but the interpretation is broader. Area under a speed–time graph gives distance travelled when speed is nonnegative; signed area under a velocity–time graph gives displacement. Area under a force–displacement curve gives mechanical work. The axes and their units decide what the integral means.

The simplest area to accumulate

Consider an idealised object with velocity v(t) = 2t + 1 m/s, where t is measured in seconds. The velocity starts at 1 m/s and increases by 2 m/s every second.

Displacement from 0 to 3 s = ∫₀³ (2t + 1) dt = [t² + t]₀³ = 12 m

This is an example of the fundamental theorem of calculus: integrating a rate recovers the accumulated change. Notice how it connects to our previous lesson on derivatives: the derivative of t² + t is 2t + 1.

Divide the interval into narrow pieces, approximate the function in each piece and add rectangle areas. Making the pieces narrower generally improves the approximation for a well-behaved function. The definite integral is the limiting accumulated total, not a picture that must be geometrically positive: portions below the axis contribute negative signed area.

An antiderivative offers a powerful shortcut. The fundamental theorem of calculus connects the accumulation from a to b with F(b)−F(a), where F is a function whose derivative is f. For example, ∫ from 0 to 3 of 2t dt equals [t²] from 0 to 3 = 9. If the integrand is velocity in m/s, the answer is displacement in metres. Always distinguish an indefinite integral (a family of antiderivatives) from a definite integral (a numerical accumulation).

Worked examples

Problem: Water flows into a tank at Q(t) = 3 + 2t litres/minute. How much enters in the first 4 minutes?
  1. Find the accumulated volume: V = ∫₀⁴ (3 + 2t) dt.
  2. Integrate: V = [3t + t²]₀⁴.
  3. Evaluate: V = 12 + 16 = 28 litres.

The same mathematics handles distance, accumulated rainfall, electric charge and many other quantities.

Worked example 2

Question. A tank fills at Q(t) = 2t L/min. How much water enters between 1 and 4 minutes?

Solution. Integrate: ∫₁⁴ 2t dt = [t²]₁⁴ = 16 − 1 = 15 L.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5
Your turn: A cart moves with velocity v(t) = 4 + t m/s from t = 0 to 5 s. Find its displacement.
Show worked answer

∫₀⁵ (4 + t) dt = [4t + t²/2]₀⁵ = 20 + 12.5 = 32.5 m. Since v is positive throughout, that is also the distance travelled.

Problem 2 of 5

Evaluate ∫₀³ 4 dx.

Show answer and method

4 × 3 = 12.

Problem 3 of 5

Find an antiderivative of 6x.

Show answer and method

3x² + C.

Problem 4 of 5

A constant flow of 5 L/min lasts 12 min. How much passes?

Show answer and method

Area under flow curve = 5 × 12 = 60 L.

Problem 5 of 5

Can a definite integral be negative?

Show answer and method

Yes. For example ∫₀² −3 dx = −6.

Try it yourself

Sketch a velocity–time graph for a toy car that moves steadily for 2 seconds, then moves twice as fast for 2 seconds. Divide the graph into rectangles. Can you calculate total displacement without using a calculus formula?

Extension: If velocity goes below zero, positive and negative areas cancel when you calculate displacement; for total distance, add their absolute magnitudes.
