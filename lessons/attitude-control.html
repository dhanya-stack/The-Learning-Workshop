Attitude Control and Reaction Wheels

Why can a spinning wheel help turn a spacecraft without pushing against air?

Keeping a spacecraft pointed

Attitude is spacecraft orientation. Reaction wheels store angular momentum; speeding up a wheel causes the spacecraft body to rotate in the opposite sense, preserving total momentum when external torque is negligible. Sensors estimate orientation and controllers issue commands.

Orbit describes where a satellite travels; attitude describes which direction it points. A telescope can follow the right orbit yet miss its target if its pointing is wrong. Sensors estimate orientation, actuators create torques, and control algorithms decide how much correction is needed.

A reaction wheel is a rotating mass that stores angular momentum. Accelerating the wheel applies a reaction torque to the satellite in the opposite direction, allowing fine pointing without continuously expelling propellant. Over time, environmental torques can cause wheels to accumulate momentum and require unloading.

Torque, momentum and control

In a simplified one-axis system, angular momentum is Iω. If a reaction wheel changes momentum by ΔH, the spacecraft body takes an approximately equal and opposite change, unless external torques intervene.

ΔH_body ≈ −ΔH_wheel

For a simplified single-axis system, angular momentum conservation means a change in wheel angular momentum produces an opposing change in spacecraft angular momentum, neglecting external torques. The spacecraft rotational response depends on its moment of inertia. Magnetic torquers can interact with Earth's magnetic field to unload momentum in some orbits.

Pointing precision is limited by sensor noise, actuator capacity, disturbances and structural flexing. Fast wheel commands can excite flexible appendages. The control system must balance responsiveness with stability, while attitude knowledge and attitude control accuracy remain distinct performance quantities.

Worked examples

Question. A reaction wheel changes its angular momentum by +0.12 N·m·s. Approximately how much does spacecraft body momentum change?

Solution, step by step. Conservation gives ΔH_body ≈ −0.12 N·m·s: equal magnitude, opposite sign, neglecting external torque. The final angular speed also depends on body inertia.

Worked example 2

Question. A reaction wheel changes its angular momentum by 0.08 N·m·s in 4 s. Find average torque magnitude exchanged with the satellite.

Solution. Average torque magnitude =|ΔH|/Δt=0.08/4=0.02 N·m; spacecraft torque is opposite wheel change.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. If the spacecraft has inertia 6 kg·m², what angular-speed change corresponds to body momentum −0.12 N·m·s?

Show worked answer

Δω = ΔH/I = −0.12/6 = −0.020 rad/s.

Problem 2 of 5

What is a reaction wheel used for?

Show answer and method

Changing spacecraft attitude by exchanging angular momentum.

Problem 3 of 5

A constant 0.01 N·m torque for 5 s changes angular momentum by how much?

Show answer and method

0.05 N·m·s.

Problem 4 of 5

Can a reaction wheel continuously remove total system angular momentum without external torque?

Show answer and method

No; desaturation generally requires external torques, e.g. thrusters or magnetorquers.

Problem 5 of 5

What sensor can measure angular rate?

Show answer and method

Gyroscope.

Try it yourself

Sit on a swivel chair holding a small spinning wheel under safe supervision, or use a simulation to study opposite rotations.

Where this model stops

Reaction wheels can saturate; real spacecraft often need external torque sources to unload stored momentum.
