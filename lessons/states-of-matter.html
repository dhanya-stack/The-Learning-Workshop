States of Matter and Phase Changes

Why does water boil at a lower temperature on a mountain?

Why matter changes state

A phase change occurs when conditions favour a different arrangement of matter. Solid, liquid and gas states differ in molecular freedom and interactions. Boiling begins when the liquid’s vapour pressure matches the surrounding pressure.

Ice, liquid water and water vapour contain the same H₂O molecules. What changes is how those molecules are arranged and how much freedom they have to move relative to one another. In a solid, particles are usually constrained around relatively fixed positions; in a liquid they can rearrange; in a gas they move through much larger available space.

Temperature and pressure together affect which phase is stable. This is why boiling temperature changes at altitude and why substances can be liquefied through pressure and cooling. A phase change can absorb or release energy even while the temperature remains approximately constant during the transition.

Energy, particles and phase changes

Heat supplied during a phase change may change the fraction of each phase rather than temperature. For a simplified constant-pressure change, required latent heat equals mass times specific latent heat.

Q = mL (latent heat at a specified phase transition)

Heating a substance raises internal energy, but the energy may go into changing temperature or into changing phase. During melting or boiling of a pure substance at constant pressure, added energy can supply latent heat rather than produce an immediate temperature rise. For mass m, an idealised latent-heat calculation uses Q=mL.

A phase diagram shows where different phases are stable as pressure and temperature vary. The triple point is where three phases coexist in equilibrium, and the critical point marks the end of the distinct liquid–vapour coexistence boundary. Real materials, mixtures and nonequilibrium processes can complicate this simple picture.

Worked examples

Question. How much energy is needed to melt 0.20 kg of ice at 0°C? Take latent heat of fusion as 334 kJ/kg.

Solution, step by step. Q = 0.20 × 334 = 66.8 kJ. The ice changes phase at approximately constant temperature, assuming standard pressure.

Worked example 2

Question. Estimate energy to melt 0.2 kg ice at 0°C if latent heat of fusion is 334 kJ/kg.

Solution. Q=mL=0.2×334=66.8 kJ; temperature stays ~0°C during melting at fixed pressure.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. Estimate energy to melt 0.05 kg of ice at 0°C using the same value.

Show worked answer

Q = 0.05 × 334 = 16.7 kJ.

Problem 2 of 5

Energy to melt 0.1 kg ice using 334 kJ/kg?

Show answer and method

33.4 kJ.

Problem 3 of 5

At constant pressure, does a pure substance normally change temperature while melting?

Show answer and method

Not while the phase change proceeds in equilibrium.

Problem 4 of 5

What is sublimation?

Show answer and method

Direct conversion from solid to gas.

Problem 5 of 5

Why can evaporation cool skin?

Show answer and method

Higher-energy molecules escape, removing energy from remaining liquid and skin.

Try it yourself

Watch ice melt at room temperature and record temperature over time using a suitable thermometer. Distinguish warming from melting.

Where this model stops

Do not use a constant latent heat far outside its stated conditions; actual boiling point changes with external pressure.
