Pressure in fluids

Explore how water depth and fluid density affect pressure, then try a simple observation at home.

Why does the water push harder deeper down?

Pressure measures how strongly a force acts over an area. In a still fluid, each deeper layer supports the weight of the fluid above it. That is why pressure rises with depth.

Pressure = Force / Area     p = F/A

For a liquid of approximately constant density, the increase in pressure below its surface is:

Δp = ρgh

Here ρ is density (kg/m³), g is gravitational acceleration (about 9.81 m/s² on Earth), and h is depth (m). The answer is in pascals (Pa). This is gauge pressure relative to the surface, not total absolute pressure.

Pressure is force distributed over area. A drawing pin can pierce material because its sharp tip concentrates the same applied force onto a tiny area. In a stationary liquid, however, pressure at a point acts in all directions and normally increases as you move downward because the water above has weight.

You can feel this when diving a short distance in a swimming pool. The increase depends on depth and the liquid's density, not the shape of the pool. An unusually wide lake and a narrow tall column of the same liquid produce the same hydrostatic pressure at equal depth below their free surfaces, assuming the same atmospheric pressure.

Pressure acts in every direction

In a fluid at rest, pressure pushes normal to any surface—even a vertical wall. A deeper point experiences greater pressure, but two points at the same depth in the same connected liquid have equal pressure.

Water density is roughly 1000 kg/m³; oil is often less dense. At the same depth, lower-density oil creates a smaller pressure increase.

For a uniform incompressible fluid at rest, the gauge-pressure increase over a vertical depth h is ρgh. The absolute pressure is atmospheric pressure plus that increase when the surface is exposed to air. At 2 m depth in freshwater, the gauge pressure is approximately 1000×9.81×2=19.6 kPa.

Hydrostatic pressure gives rise to forces on walls and gates. Because pressure changes with depth, the load on a vertical panel is not generally uniform: lower portions carry more pressure. Integrating pressure over area determines the total force and its point of action. The simple equation is not a complete description for rapidly moving fluids or liquids whose density changes significantly with depth.

Worked examples

Problem: What is the gauge pressure 2.0 m below the surface of fresh water?
  1. Take ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s², h = 2.0 m.
  2. Δp = 1000 × 9.81 × 2.0 = 19,620 Pa.
  3. Convert to kilopascals: 19.62 kPa.

If the surface is exposed to the atmosphere, absolute pressure is approximately atmospheric pressure plus 19.62 kPa.

Worked example 2

Question. Find gauge pressure 5 m under fresh water (ρ=1000 kg/m³; g=9.81 m/s²).

Solution. p=ρgh=1000×9.81×5=49,050 Pa≈49.1 kPa.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5
Your turn: What is the gauge pressure 3 m deep in oil of density 800 kg/m³?
Show worked answer

Δp = 800 × 9.81 × 3 = 23,544 Pa = 23.54 kPa.

Problem 2 of 5

Gauge pressure at 1 m under water? Use g=10 m/s².

Show answer and method

10,000 Pa = 10 kPa.

Problem 3 of 5

If depth doubles, what happens to hydrostatic gauge pressure for constant density?

Show answer and method

It doubles.

Problem 4 of 5

Find pressure force on 0.2 m² at uniform pressure 20 kPa.

Show answer and method

F=pA=20,000×0.2=4,000 N.

Problem 5 of 5

Why is pressure at one depth the same in every direction in a fluid at rest?

Show answer and method

A static fluid cannot support shear; otherwise it would deform and flow.

A simple bottle experiment

For a supervised demonstration, have an adult prepare two small holes at different heights in a disposable plastic bottle using appropriate tools before the activity. Place the bottle over a sink or tray, add water, and compare the water streams. Do not use glass containers or handle sharp tools without supervision. The stream trajectories also depend on changing water level and hole geometry.

Try explaining: Why doesn't a wider tank automatically create more pressure at the same depth?
