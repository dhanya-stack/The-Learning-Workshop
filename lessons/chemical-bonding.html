Chemical bonds: sharing or transferring electrons

Why do atoms join together? Compare ionic and covalent bonding through familiar examples and simple electron diagrams.

Why atoms form bonds

Atoms can reach lower-energy, more stable arrangements when they interact with other atoms. The valence electrons—electrons in the outermost occupied region—are especially important in predicting how atoms bond.

Two useful beginner models are ionic bonding, in which oppositely charged ions attract, and covalent bonding, in which atoms share electron density. Real chemical bonding involves electrons described by quantum mechanics; these sketches are simplified learning models.

Atoms bond because certain electron arrangements can lower the total energy of the combined system. The common classroom categories—ionic, covalent and metallic—are useful models, but many real bonds have characteristics that lie between ideal categories. The chemical properties of a substance depend strongly on how its atoms and electrons are organised.

Table salt is built from a lattice of oppositely charged ions; water contains polar covalent bonds within molecules; copper has mobile electrons that help it conduct electricity. These structural differences explain why materials with similar-looking atoms can have radically different melting temperatures, hardness and electrical behaviour.

Ionic and covalent bonds

Sodium chloride: a sodium atom can lose an electron while a chlorine atom gains one. Their ions, Na⁺ and Cl⁻, attract one another. Solid table salt forms an extended ionic lattice, not separate NaCl molecules.

Hydrogen gas: two hydrogen atoms each contribute an electron to a shared pair, forming H₂. In hydrogen chloride (HCl), electrons are shared unequally: chlorine attracts them more strongly, creating a polar covalent bond.

Ionic bonding involves electrostatic attraction between ions in an extended structure. Covalent bonding involves electron density shared between nuclei; it may be distributed unequally if the atoms have different electronegativities. In metals, bonding is often described through delocalised electrons associated with the metal lattice.

A bond has an energy cost to break, but breaking bonds alone does not release energy. A chemical reaction's net energy change depends on energy required to break old bonds versus energy released when new bonds form. Also distinguish bonds within molecules from intermolecular forces between molecules; water's boiling behaviour depends strongly on intermolecular hydrogen bonding, not on breaking the O–H covalent bonds in each molecule.

Electron-picture model

Worked examples

Problem: Sodium has one valence electron; chlorine has seven. Describe the simplest ionic picture of sodium chloride.
  1. Sodium loses one electron and becomes Na⁺.
  2. Chlorine gains one electron and becomes Cl⁻.
  3. Opposite charges attract, and many ions arrange into an ionic crystal lattice.

Electron transfer helps us understand the ion charges. An actual crystal's stability comes from the combined electrostatic interactions throughout the lattice.

Worked example 2

Question. Explain how a covalent bond forms between two hydrogen atoms.

Solution. Each H supplies one valence electron. They share a pair, allowing each to attain a two-electron duet in H₂. The bonding involves electron density between nuclei.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5
Your turn: Each hydrogen atom has one electron. How many electrons are in the shared pair of H₂, and what type of bond is it?
Show worked answer

There are two electrons in one shared pair. H₂ has a nonpolar covalent bond because the two identical atoms share equally.

Problem 2 of 5

What bonding dominates crystalline sodium chloride?

Show answer and method

Ionic electrostatic attraction.

Problem 3 of 5

How many electron pairs form a single covalent bond?

Show answer and method

One shared pair.

Problem 4 of 5

Is a metal usually held together by isolated electron-pair bonds between fixed atom pairs?

Show answer and method

No; metallic bonding involves delocalised electrons.

Problem 5 of 5

Why can graphite conduct electricity while diamond generally cannot?

Show answer and method

Graphite has mobile delocalised electrons within layers; diamond bonds localise valence electrons.
