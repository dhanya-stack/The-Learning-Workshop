Electrons and Atomic Orbitals

What does it really mean when we say an electron is “around” a nucleus?

Where are electrons likely to be?

Electrons in atoms are described by quantum states, not miniature planets following fixed paths. An orbital describes a spatial probability distribution associated with a quantum state. It helps predict bonding and chemical properties.

Electrons do not have simultaneously definite positions and momenta in the way small classical balls do. An orbital describes a quantum state and provides a probability distribution for where an electron might be detected. The familiar s, p and d shapes represent patterns in probability density, not solid containers holding electrons.

The idea becomes useful when we ask why atoms form particular bonds or why certain electronic transitions occur. Electrons occupy allowed states with specific energies and quantum numbers, constrained by the Pauli exclusion principle. These rules give rise to the arrangements represented schematically in the periodic table.

Orbitals and quantum states

Orbitals are labelled s, p, d and f with different shapes and capacities. Each spatial orbital can hold at most two electrons with opposite spin. Electron filling follows energy ordering and the Pauli exclusion principle, with some important exceptions.

one orbital → maximum 2 electrons; p subshell → 3 orbitals → maximum 6 electrons

The principal quantum number n relates to the shell, the angular-momentum quantum number l to subshell type, and the magnetic quantum number m_l to orbital orientation. Electron spin provides another quantum number. A single spatial orbital can accommodate at most two electrons with opposite spins.

For simple many-electron atoms, Aufbau filling rules and Hund's rule are useful starting points, but the actual electron configurations of some elements show exceptions. Orbital diagrams are not direct experimental pictures. It is more accurate to think of calculated states that help explain measured properties such as spectra, ionisation energies and bonding behaviour.

Worked examples

Question. Write the ground-state electron configuration of a neutral oxygen atom (Z = 8).

Solution, step by step. Place 2 electrons in 1s, 2 in 2s and the remaining 4 in 2p: 1s² 2s² 2p⁴. Its outer shell contains six electrons.

Worked example 2

Question. Write the electron configuration of oxygen (Z=8) and count its valence electrons.

Solution. Fill lowest-energy orbitals: 1s² 2s² 2p⁴. Valence shell n=2 has 2+4=6 electrons.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. How many electrons can fill the three p orbitals of a single shell?

Show worked answer

Each orbital holds at most two; 3 × 2 = 6 electrons.

Problem 2 of 5

Maximum electrons in an s subshell?

Show answer and method

2.

Problem 3 of 5

Maximum electrons in a p subshell?

Show answer and method

6.

Problem 4 of 5

What is the ground-state configuration of helium?

Show answer and method

1s².

Problem 5 of 5

Can two electrons in one orbital have identical spin quantum numbers?

Show answer and method

No; paired electrons have opposite spins.
