Gravity and Escape Velocity

Why can a spacecraft leave Earth without continuing to fire its engine forever?

Can an object escape gravity?

Gravity weakens with distance, and gaining altitude increases gravitational potential energy. Escape speed is the minimum starting speed needed for an unpowered object to reach arbitrarily far away with zero leftover speed in a simplified two-body model.

Throw a ball upward and Earth's gravity slows it until it reaches a highest point. Give it more initial speed and it rises higher. Escape speed is the ideal threshold at which an object can move indefinitely far from a gravitating body without needing more propulsion, ending with zero speed at infinity in the simplified model.

It is not a rule that rockets must immediately exceed escape speed at ground level. A rocket can climb and accelerate under sustained thrust, and mission planning may use parking orbits and later manoeuvres. Escape energy is a statement about the total mechanical energy of a trajectory, not a universal launch instruction.

Energy and escape speed

Set initial kinetic energy equal to the magnitude of gravitational binding energy. The object’s own mass cancels, so escape speed depends on the attracting body’s mass and launch radius, not on spacecraft mass. Real launches also contend with atmosphere and Earth rotation.

v_escape = √(2GM/r)

For a spherical central mass M, the specific mechanical energy of an object is ε=v²/2−GM/r. Setting ε=0 gives the local escape speed v_esc=sqrt(2GM/r). The expression assumes no atmospheric drag, no other gravitational bodies and no additional propulsion after the initial condition.

Because orbital speed for a circular orbit at the same radius is sqrt(GM/r), local escape speed is sqrt(2) times circular speed. The radius is measured from the centre of the gravitating body. In real missions, atmospheric losses and gravity losses, finite burns, other-body effects and planetary rotation influence required launch performance.

Worked examples

Question. Earth’s gravitational parameter is μ = GM ≈ 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s²; Earth radius r ≈ 6.371 × 10⁶ m. Estimate escape speed at the surface.

Solution, step by step. v = √(2 × 3.986×10¹⁴ / 6.371×10⁶) ≈ 11,186 m/s ≈ 11.2 km/s. This is a vacuum idealisation, not a launch vehicle’s required single burn.

Worked example 2

Question. Calculate escape speed at a planet surface with μ=400,000 km³/s² and radius 6400 km, ignoring atmosphere/rotation.

Solution. vₑ=√(2μ/r)=√(800000/6400)=√125≈11.18 km/s.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. If a planet had the same radius as Earth but four times its mass, how would escape speed change?

Show worked answer

v_escape scales as √M at fixed radius; quadrupling mass doubles escape speed.

Problem 2 of 5

Escape speed formula in terms of μ and radius?

Show answer and method

√(2μ/r).

Problem 3 of 5

If planet mass doubles at same radius, escape speed changes by factor?

Show answer and method

√2.

Problem 4 of 5

Is reaching escape velocity the only way a powered craft can escape?

Show answer and method

No; continuous propulsion can escape without an initial instantaneous speed equal to the ballistic escape speed.

Problem 5 of 5

Does ideal ballistic escape speed depend on spacecraft mass?

Show answer and method

No.

Try it yourself

Compare the formula with circular-orbit speed v_c = √(GM/r). Why is escape speed √2 times greater at the same location?

Where this model stops

Escape speed is not the same as the rocket’s required total Δv: losses, trajectories and staging matter.
