Applications of Integration

Why can accumulated distance be read from a velocity–time graph?

What can a sum of tiny pieces tell us?

An integral adds many small contributions. If speed changes, multiplying one speed by a whole time interval may be misleading. Instead, divide time into narrow intervals and add speed × interval width.

An integral can sum any quantity that is naturally built from small contributions. A water tank with changing cross-sectional area fills at different rates as its level rises; a nonuniform rod can have different mass along its length. Rather than forcing a single average, integration handles those changes systematically.

The important first step is often deciding what the small piece represents. For mass, use a small length times linear density or a small volume times volumetric density. For work, use a small displacement times the force acting during that displacement. Drawing the situation and checking units frequently matters more than memorising the final integral.

Using integrals to measure accumulated change

For velocity v(t) = 2t (m/s) from t = 0 to 4 s, displacement equals the area under the graph. Integration is an exact limit of the rectangle-addition idea. The same method calculates work from a variable force and area under a curve.

displacement = ∫₀⁴ 2t dt = [t²]₀⁴

Suppose a force varies with position as F(x)=3x newtons, with x in metres. The work done from x=0 to x=2 m is ∫₀²3x dx = [1.5x²]₀²=6 J. Using the final force 6 N over the full distance would give 12 J and overestimate the work because the force was smaller earlier.

Integration can also find the volume of a solid by stacking thin slices: volume equals the sum of cross-sectional area A(x) times thickness dx. In a physical problem, make sure every contribution is counted exactly once and that the integration limits correspond to the actual system. When analytical integration is inconvenient, numerical methods approximate the same accumulation.

Worked examples

Question. A cart has v(t) = 2t m/s. How far does it move from 0 to 4 seconds?

Solution, step by step. Antiderivative of 2t is t². Evaluate at the bounds: 4² − 0² = 16 m. Units confirm the result: (m/s) × s = m.

Worked example 2

Question. A spring requires F(x)=100x N to stretch x metres. Find work from 0 to 0.2 m.

Solution. Work = ∫₀^0.2 100x dx = [50x²]₀^0.2 = 2 J.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. For velocity v(t) = 3t² m/s, find displacement from t = 0 to 2 s.

Show worked answer

∫₀² 3t² dt = [t³]₀² = 8 m.

Problem 2 of 5

Find area under y=3 from x=0 to x=5.

Show answer and method

15 square units.

Problem 3 of 5

Find signed area ∫₀² x dx.

Show answer and method

[x²/2]₀² = 2.

Problem 4 of 5

A cart moves at v=3 m/s for 4 s. Distance?

Show answer and method

∫ v dt = 12 m.

Problem 5 of 5

Does integrating velocity always give distance travelled?

Show answer and method

No; integral gives signed displacement. Integrate speed |v| to get distance.

Try it yourself

Approximate the triangular area below v = 2t from 0 to 4 using four wide rectangles and then eight narrower ones. Compare with 16 m.

Where this model stops

Signed area gives displacement, not always total distance: negative velocity contributes negative displacement.
