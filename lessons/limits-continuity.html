Limits and Continuity

How do we describe a value we are approaching but may never reach?

What happens when we get arbitrarily close?

A limit concerns the behaviour near a point, not necessarily the value at the point. Continuity means the limiting value agrees with the actual function value, so the graph can be traced locally without a jump or hole.

A limit asks what value a function approaches as its input gets close to a chosen point. It need not ask for the value exactly at that point. This distinction matters when a formula contains a division by zero at one point but behaves perfectly predictably nearby. The limit captures that nearby behaviour.

Picture driving toward a junction: knowing your distance from the junction as you get closer is a different question from whether you actually enter it. Similarly, a graph can approach one height without having a plotted point at that height. A discontinuity can look like a hole, a sudden step, or an unbounded rise. These shapes represent different mathematical situations.

Limits and continuity

Take f(x) = (x² − 4)/(x − 2). It is undefined at x = 2, yet everywhere else it equals x + 2. Values on both sides of 2 approach 4. This is a removable hole, not a mysterious infinity.

lim(x→2) (x² − 4)/(x − 2) = 4

A function is continuous at x=a when three requirements hold together: f(a) exists, the limit as x approaches a exists, and the limit equals f(a). Both left-hand and right-hand approaches must agree for a two-sided limit. For example, (x²−1)/(x−1) equals x+1 whenever x≠1. The original expression is undefined at x=1, but its limit there is 2.

Limits form the foundation of derivatives and integrals. The slope of a secant line approaches a tangent slope through a limit; increasingly fine sums approach an integral through a limit. A limit may fail to exist if the left and right behaviours disagree or if the function oscillates without approaching one number.

Worked examples

Question. Find the limit as x approaches 3 of (x² − 9)/(x − 3).

Solution, step by step. Factor the numerator: (x − 3)(x + 3). For x different from 3, cancel the common factor to get x + 3. The approaching value is 6.

Worked example 2

Question. Find lim(x→2) (x²−4)/(x−2).

Solution. Factor numerator: (x−2)(x+2)/(x−2) = x+2 for x≠2. Limit = 4.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. What is lim(x→5) (x² − 25)/(x − 5)? Is the original formula defined at x = 5?

Show worked answer

The limit is 10; the original expression is undefined at x = 5 because of division by zero.

Problem 2 of 5

Evaluate lim(x→3) (2x+1).

Show answer and method

7.

Problem 3 of 5

What is lim(x→0) x²?

Show answer and method

0.

Problem 4 of 5

Is f(x)=1/x continuous at x=0?

Show answer and method

No; it is not defined at x=0.

Problem 5 of 5

Can a function have a limit at a point where it is undefined?

Show answer and method

Yes: (x²−1)/(x−1) has limit 2 at x=1 despite being undefined there.

Try it yourself

Evaluate the function close to the missing point, from both sides, such as 1.9 and 2.1. Compare the trend with the factored expression.

Where this model stops

Cancelling a factor identifies the nearby behaviour, but it does not make the original function defined at its hole.
