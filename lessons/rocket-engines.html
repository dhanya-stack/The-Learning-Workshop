Rocket Propulsion and Engines

How does expelling hot gas create thrust even in the vacuum of space?

How a rocket engine produces thrust

Rocket engines accelerate propellant backward; the reaction force accelerates the vehicle forward. The exhaust pushes on the vehicle’s internal surfaces before leaving. No atmosphere is required for rocket thrust.

A rocket engine creates a high-speed exhaust jet, but there are many ways to produce and accelerate that jet. Chemical rockets release energy through reactions and expand hot gas through a nozzle. Electric thrusters use electrical energy to accelerate propellant and can operate efficiently with small thrust over long periods.

The appropriate engine depends on the mission. A launch vehicle needs substantial thrust to overcome gravity and atmospheric forces; a deep-space probe may favour high specific impulse even if it accelerates very slowly. Comparing two engines using only exhaust temperature or only thrust misses much of the picture.

Propellants, exhaust and efficiency

In a simplified steady rocket, thrust comes from exhaust momentum and any pressure mismatch between nozzle exit and ambient. Propulsion systems vary: chemical engines offer high thrust, electric propulsion often offers high exhaust velocity but low thrust.

F = ṁvₑ + (pₑ − pₐ)Aₑ

Momentum flow through an engine provides thrust. In a chemical nozzle, hot gas converts some thermal energy into directed exhaust kinetic energy; the pressure difference between exit and surroundings may contribute additional force. The nozzle's performance changes with surrounding pressure, so geometry optimised for sea level need not be optimal in vacuum.

Specific impulse I_sp is thrust divided by propellant weight-flow rate and is commonly expressed in seconds. It is related to effective exhaust velocity by v_e=I_sp g₀. High I_sp means less propellant for a given ideal Δv, but does not guarantee adequate thrust or low total system mass. Pumps, combustion stability, cooling, feed systems and control are major parts of practical engine design.

Worked examples

Question. A rocket ejects 5 kg/s of propellant at 2500 m/s. Ignore pressure thrust. Estimate force.

Solution, step by step. Momentum thrust F = ṁvₑ = 5 × 2500 = 12,500 N = 12.5 kN. Over one second, the exhaust carries 12,500 kg·m/s of momentum.

Worked example 2

Question. Estimate ideal nozzle thrust from mass flow 3 kg/s, exhaust speed 2200 m/s, neglecting pressure thrust.

Solution. F≈ṁve=3×2200=6600 N.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. An idealised electric thruster expels 0.002 kg/s at 20,000 m/s. Ignore pressure correction. Find thrust.

Show worked answer

F = 0.002 × 20,000 = 40 N.

Problem 2 of 5

For a rocket nozzle, what produces momentum thrust?

Show answer and method

Accelerating and ejecting propellant mass.

Problem 3 of 5

What extra term appears if exit pressure differs from ambient?

Show answer and method

(pₑ−pₐ)Aₑ.

Problem 4 of 5

Does exhaust speed usually rise or fall through a properly expanded supersonic nozzle?

Show answer and method

Rise as pressure/thermal energy converts into directed kinetic energy.

Problem 5 of 5

Why might vacuum and sea-level thrust differ?

Show answer and method

Ambient pressure changes the nozzle pressure-thrust contribution.
