Circular Motion and Gravitation

Why is a changing direction itself a kind of acceleration?

Why curved motion needs a force

Moving around a circle at constant speed still changes the velocity vector. The acceleration points inward, toward the centre. Gravity can provide this inward acceleration for planets and satellites; a string can provide it for a whirling mass.

When a stone moves around on a string, its speed may remain constant while its direction changes continuously. That change is acceleration, directed inward toward the circle's centre. The string supplies the necessary inward force; without it, the stone would move approximately along a straight tangent rather than flying outward along the original radius.

Gravity plays an analogous role in satellite motion. The satellite is continually falling toward Earth, yet has enough sideways velocity to keep missing the surface. Circular motion is therefore a useful bridge between everyday mechanics and orbital motion.

Centripetal acceleration

For uniform circular motion, centripetal acceleration is v²/r. Multiply by mass to find required inward net force. For orbiting objects, gravity supplies this force.

a_c = v²/r; F_c = mv²/r

For uniform circular motion of radius r at speed v, inward acceleration has magnitude a_c=v²/r, so the net inward force needed is mv²/r. It is not an extra “centripetal force” added to a free-body diagram: tension, gravity, friction or another real interaction supplies the inward resultant.

For a car taking a flat bend of radius 50 m at 10 m/s, the required lateral acceleration is 2 m/s². Tyre–road friction supplies the sideways force. If road conditions deteriorate, the maximum available friction can become insufficient. Angular speed ω=v/r, and for steady circular motion the period T=2π/ω. These relationships assume approximately circular travel, not arbitrary curving paths.

Worked examples

Question. A 0.5 kg object moves at 4 m/s in a horizontal circle of radius 2 m. Find inward force.

Solution, step by step. a_c = 4²/2 = 8 m/s². Inward force = ma_c = 0.5 × 8 = 4 N.

Worked example 2

Question. A 2 kg mass goes at 6 m/s around radius 3 m. Find centripetal force.

Solution. F=mv²/r=2×36/3=24 N, directed toward the circle centre.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A car follows a curve of radius 50 m at 10 m/s. Find required centripetal acceleration.

Show worked answer

a_c = v²/r = 100/50 = 2 m/s² inward.

Problem 2 of 5

Find centripetal acceleration for v=10 m/s, r=5 m.

Show answer and method

a=v²/r=20 m/s².

Problem 3 of 5

If circular speed doubles at fixed radius, required centripetal acceleration changes by what factor?

Show answer and method

4.

Problem 4 of 5

Does centripetal force point tangentially?

Show answer and method

No; radially inward.

Problem 5 of 5

What provides inward acceleration for a satellite in orbit?

Show answer and method

Gravity.

Try it yourself

Draw velocity arrows tangent to a circular path at several positions. Compare the change between neighbouring arrows.

Where this model stops

Centripetal force is the name for the inward net force, not a separate force to add to gravity or tension.
