Numerical Methods and Approximation

What if a useful equation cannot be solved neatly by algebra?

When an exact answer is difficult

Numerical methods use repeated approximate calculations to approach an answer. They are essential for realistic simulations, but a numerical answer still needs a convergence and error check.

Many important equations cannot be rearranged neatly to give an exact answer. Engineers still need an answer that is accurate enough to guide a decision. Numerical methods approach the result with controlled approximations, often through repeated calculations.

An approximation is not necessarily inferior to an exact-looking formula. A numerical model that accounts for relevant boundary conditions can predict a physical system better than a simple analytic formula based on unrealistic assumptions. The responsibility is to understand error and verify the result, rather than accepting the computer's digits at face value.

Approximations and numerical error

The bisection method finds a root when a continuous function changes sign over an interval. Evaluate the midpoint, keep the half containing a sign change, and repeat. Each iteration halves the bracketing interval.

If f(a) and f(b) have opposite signs, bisect [a,b] until the root is bracketed tightly.

Bisection begins with a continuous function that changes sign on an interval. The intermediate value theorem guarantees at least one root somewhere inside. Evaluate the midpoint, retain the subinterval with a sign change, and repeat. After n steps, the bracket width is the original width divided by 2^n.

This makes the stopping criterion meaningful. If the bracket is only 0.001 wide, the midpoint's absolute error is no more than 0.0005 relative to some root in the bracket, assuming the root remains bracketed. Other numerical methods, such as Newton's method, may converge faster but require additional conditions and can fail with a poor starting point. Every algorithm needs checks for accuracy, units and exceptional cases.

Worked examples

Question. Estimate √2 by solving f(x) = x² − 2 = 0 between 1 and 2.

Solution, step by step. At 1.5, f = +0.25, so retain [1,1.5]. At 1.25, f = −0.4375, so retain [1.25,1.5]. At 1.375, f = −0.1094, so retain [1.375,1.5]. The root is near 1.414.

Worked example 2

Question. Use one Newton step for f(x)=x²−2, starting at x₀=1.5.

Solution. f=0.25 and f′=3. Update x₁=1.5−0.25/3≈1.4167; √2≈1.4142.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. For f(x) = x² − 9, bracket the positive root between 2 and 4. What midpoint is the exact root?

Show worked answer

The first midpoint is (2+4)/2 = 3; f(3) = 0, so the positive root is 3.

Problem 2 of 5

Approximate f′(2) for f(x)=x² with forward difference h=0.1.

Show answer and method

[f(2.1)−f(2)]/0.1=(4.41−4)/0.1=4.1.

Problem 3 of 5

Use the trapezoidal rule for y=x from 0 to 2, one interval.

Show answer and method

(2−0)(0+2)/2 = 2.

Problem 4 of 5

If a bisection interval is [1,3], what is its midpoint?

Show answer and method

2.

Problem 5 of 5

Why does reducing the step size not always improve a numerical answer indefinitely?

Show answer and method

Roundoff error and instability can eventually dominate discretisation error.

Try it yourself

Write a few lines of Python to repeat bisection until the interval is less than 0.001 wide; print each interval.

Where this model stops

Bisection relies on continuity and an initial sign change; it cannot find every kind of root from arbitrary brackets.
