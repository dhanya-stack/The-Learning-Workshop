Properties of Fluids

Why does honey flow more slowly than water, and why do droplets bead up?

What makes one fluid different from another?

Fluids can deform continuously under shear. Density describes mass per volume, dynamic viscosity describes resistance to shearing flow, and surface tension describes energy associated with creating liquid surface. They influence different phenomena.

Water, honey and air are all fluids: they continue to deform when subjected to shear stress rather than retaining one permanent shape like an elastic solid. Yet they behave very differently because of density, viscosity, compressibility and surface tension. Each property answers a different physical question.

Density relates mass to volume and helps determine hydrostatic pressure and buoyancy. Viscosity describes resistance to shear and strongly affects flow losses. Surface tension originates from molecular interactions at a liquid interface and becomes particularly important for droplets, capillary effects and small-scale flows.

Density, viscosity and surface tension

Density ρ = m/V. For a Newtonian liquid in simple shear, shear stress is proportional to the velocity gradient with dynamic viscosity μ as the constant. Surface tension σ acts along the liquid interface, influencing droplets and capillary rise.

ρ = m/V; τ = μ (du/dy)

Dynamic viscosity μ connects shear stress to velocity gradient in a Newtonian fluid: τ=μ(du/dy). Water and air are approximately Newtonian over many common conditions, while some paints, slurries and food products are not. Kinematic viscosity ν=μ/ρ combines viscosity with density and has units m²/s.

For flow around an object or through a pipe, the Reynolds number compares inertia with viscosity, typically Re=ρvL/μ. It is dimensionless; its interpretation depends on geometry and the flow problem. Viscosity is also temperature dependent: liquid viscosity generally decreases as temperature rises, while gas viscosity often increases. Never assume that one room-temperature property value is adequate for a wide operating range.

Worked examples

Question. A sample of liquid has mass 1.2 kg and volume 0.0015 m³. Find its density.

Solution, step by step. ρ = 1.2/0.0015 = 800 kg/m³. It is less dense than water at ordinary room conditions.

Worked example 2

Question. A liquid has mass 3 kg and volume 0.002 m³. Find density.

Solution. ρ=m/V=3/0.002=1500 kg/m³.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. A liquid has mass 450 g and volume 500 mL. Calculate density in g/mL.

Show worked answer

ρ = 450/500 = 0.90 g/mL.

Problem 2 of 5

Mass of 2 L of water at 1000 kg/m³?

Show answer and method

2 kg, since 2 L = 0.002 m³.

Problem 3 of 5

Which flows more easily under equal geometry and driving force: higher- or lower-viscosity liquid?

Show answer and method

Generally lower viscosity.

Problem 4 of 5

Give one effect caused by surface tension.

Show answer and method

Formation of small droplets or capillary effects.

Problem 5 of 5

Why must fluid temperature be stated when specifying viscosity?

Show answer and method

Viscosity changes with temperature.

Try it yourself

Time equal volumes of water and syrup flowing down the same gentle slope. Put a droplet of water on a clean and waxy surface.

Where this model stops

Flow time is not a direct viscosity measurement unless geometry and driving conditions are controlled.
