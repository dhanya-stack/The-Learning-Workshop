Functions and Graphs

What can a graph tell you about a moving lift?

A graph tells a story about a relationship

A function is a rule pairing each input with one output. A graph turns that rule into a picture: the horizontal coordinate is the input, and the vertical coordinate is the result. The shape often conveys more than a table of numbers.

A function is a rule that takes an allowed input and assigns exactly one output. In practical work, the input might be elapsed time and the output could be the temperature of a cooling component. A graph lets us see a whole relationship, including trends that would be hard to recognise in a table of numbers.

Different graphs answer different questions. A straight line indicates a constant rate of change; a curved line suggests the rate itself changes. A sudden jump might represent switching a heater on, but it might also mean the mathematical model is missing intermediate behaviour. Always check what the horizontal and vertical axes actually measure before interpreting the shape.

Inputs, outputs and functions

For a lift rising steadily, height can be modelled by h(t) = 1.5t + 2, with t in seconds and h in metres. The intercept 2 is the starting height; the slope 1.5 is metres gained per second. A curved graph instead suggests changing rate.

h(t) = 1.5t + 2

The domain specifies allowed inputs and the range specifies resulting outputs. A reciprocal function such as y=1/x excludes x=0; a square-root model y=sqrt(x) requires x≥0 in the real numbers. Real-world restrictions can be even tighter: a negative pipe diameter is not physically meaningful even if some formula accepts it.

Think about a function as a model rather than a collection of plotted points. The slope tells you a rate, the intercept gives a reference value, and the area may represent an accumulated quantity. Two different formulae can fit a small experimental dataset equally well but make different predictions outside the measured range. This is why interpolation within data and extrapolation beyond it are not equally reliable.

Worked examples

Question. Where is the lift after 6 seconds, and when does it reach 17 metres?

Solution, step by step. Insert t = 6: h(6) = 1.5 × 6 + 2 = 11 m. For 17 m, solve 17 = 1.5t + 2. Subtract 2 and divide by 1.5: t = 10 s.

Worked example 2

Question. A taxi costs ₹60 plus ₹15 per kilometre. Write the fare function and find the price at 8 km.

Solution. Let d be distance. C(d) = 60 + 15d. At d = 8, C = 60 + 120 = ₹180.

Practice set

Five questions, from quick checks to applied problems. Reveal the answer after trying each one.

Problem 1 of 5

Your turn. For d(t) = 4t + 1, find d(3) and the time when d = 25.

Show worked answer

d(3) = 13; 25 = 4t + 1 gives t = 6.

Problem 2 of 5

For f(x) = 2x + 1, find f(−3).

Show answer and method

−5.

Problem 3 of 5

What is the slope of y = −4x + 7?

Show answer and method

−4.

Problem 4 of 5

Find the x-intercept of y = 3x − 12.

Show answer and method

Set y = 0: x = 4.

Problem 5 of 5

Can one input produce two outputs in a mathematical function?

Show answer and method

No: each input in the domain has exactly one output.

Try it yourself

Make a two-column time–height table, then plot the points on squared paper. What would a horizontal line mean physically?

Where this model stops

Check the units on both axes; a graph is not meaningful without knowing what each axis represents.
