Mechanical Linkages

Make a four-bar linkage and investigate how rotating one arm moves another.

The idea

A linkage converts motion using rigid bars and joints. A four-bar mechanism consists of a fixed ground link and three moving links connected by pivots. Changing bar lengths can turn steady rotation into an oscillating motion, a useful starting point for understanding real mechanisms.

Read the step-by-step video text
  1. Cut the arms: Prepare card links with pivot spacings 4, 10 and 9 cm.
  2. Mark the base: Mark two fixed pivots 11 cm apart on a rigid card base.
  3. Add the fasteners: Join the links with four loose split pins.
  4. Rotate the input: Turn the short 4 cm crank slowly. Watch the output arm rock.
  5. Measure angles: Mark output angles for several crank positions.
  6. Experiment: Change one link length and observe how motion changes.

What you need

  1. One sheet of thin cardboard — For linkage arms.
  2. One ruler — To measure the arms.
  3. One pencil — To mark pivot locations.
  4. Safety scissors — For cutting cardboard.
  5. Four paper split pins (brads) — For the rotating joints.

Safety: Cut carefully on a table. Small metal fasteners are not suitable for unsupervised young children.

Build it

  1. Mark two fixed pivots 11 cm apart on a card base.
  2. Cut links with pivot-to-pivot lengths of 4 cm (input), 10 cm (coupler), and 9 cm (output). Leave enough material around the holes.
  3. Join the three links end-to-end, and attach their free ends to the two fixed pivots using loose paper fasteners.
  4. Slowly rotate the 4 cm input link and watch the output link rock. If the joints bind, loosen the fasteners or add spacers.

Measure and explain

Mark the output angle at several equally spaced input angles. Plot output angle against input angle. The graph is usually not a straight line: the movement is nonlinear. Try describing where the output moves rapidly and where it changes direction. The bar lengths meet the Grashof condition for a crank–rocker configuration, but cardboard flexibility and collisions affect your model.

Experiment further

Change only the coupler length and see whether the mechanism still moves through its full range. What happens near an almost-straight configuration? Compare your observations with linkage motion in machinery, folding structures and robotic devices.
