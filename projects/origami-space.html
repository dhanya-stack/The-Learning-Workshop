Origami for Space

Explore how a large surface can fold into a small package and deploy again.

The idea

Spacecraft need large solar arrays, antennas and other surfaces after reaching orbit, but those structures must fit inside a launch vehicle first. Folding and deployable mechanisms solve a packing problem. Paper models let us study compactness, geometry and repeated deployment.

Read the step-by-step video text
  1. Gather paper: Use two sheets, a ruler and pencil. Choose an easy-to-fold paper.
  2. Mark the folds: Mark parallel fold lines about two centimetres apart.
  3. Fold alternately: Alternate mountain and valley creases to make an accordion.
  4. Pack and deploy: Compress the paper, then reopen it repeatedly.
  5. Measure: Compare the deployed width with its packed width.
  6. Connect to spacecraft: Space structures also need hinges, rigidity and reliable deployment.

What you need

  1. Two equal sheets of paper — Ordinary notebook paper is suitable.
  2. One ruler — For comparing dimensions.
  3. One pencil — To mark folds or sketch patterns.

Safety: No cutting is required for the initial folding experiment.

Build it

  1. Mark equally spaced parallel lines across one sheet, about 2 cm apart.
  2. Fold along these lines, alternating mountain and valley creases, to make an accordion strip.
  3. Open and close it ten times. Note how its projected width changes.
  4. On the second sheet, sketch a slanted parallelogram grid and experiment with alternating crease directions to explore Miura-ori-inspired folding. This is a geometry exploration, not a validated spacecraft crease design.

Measure and explain

Measure the strip’s width when open and when packed. Define a simple packing ratio = deployed width ÷ folded width. If 24 cm folds to 4 cm, the ratio is 6. It describes width only, not the packed volume of a real space mechanism. Why would hinge thickness and panel rigidity change the result?

Experiment further

Try thicker paper and compare how reliably it folds. Sketch the crease pattern and label mountain and valley folds. Look for places where multiple creases meet and panels interfere; these are genuine design considerations for deployable systems.
