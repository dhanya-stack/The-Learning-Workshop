Model Rocket

Build a balloon-powered model to investigate thrust, motion and stability.

The idea

A rocket moves forward by throwing mass backwards. You can observe the same principle without combustion or a flying projectile: let an inflated balloon release air while it slides along a taut string. The air moves one way; the balloon experiences a force the other way. This is an accessible model of thrust, not a scale model of rocket propulsion.

Read the step-by-step video text

1. Make a balloon rocket. It travels along a string using escaping air, without flames or fuel.

2. Gather one balloon, one straight straw, two to three metres of string, tape, measuring tape, and a stopwatch.

3. First, pass the string through the straw. The straw acts as the moving guide.

4. Tie the string securely between two stable supports. Pull it tight, and leave a clear path.

5. Inflate the balloon and pinch its neck closed. Tape it to the straw, with the opening pointing backwards.

6. At the starting mark, release the balloon neck. Air rushes back and the balloon slides forward.

7. Measure the distance between two marks, and time the journey. Divide distance by time to estimate average speed.

8. Repeat three times. Change one thing at a time, such as balloon inflation, and compare results.

9. Keep the path clear and never point the balloon at anyone. This demonstrates thrust; it is not a powered rocket.

What you need

  1. One medium balloon — Use a latex-free alternative if necessary.
  2. One straight drinking straw — The straw becomes the sliding guide.
  3. 2–3 metres of string — Long enough for a clear path.
  4. Tape — Two small pieces to fix the balloon to the straw.
  5. Measuring tape or ruler — To mark a known distance.
  6. Stopwatch or phone timer — To record the travel time.

Safety: Work in a clear area with secure supports. Keep the balloon opening and string away from faces. Do not use fireworks, chemical propellants, pressurised bottles or heat.

Build it

  1. Thread the string through the straw before tying the string between two stable supports.
  2. Inflate a balloon, pinch the neck closed and tape its body to the straw. Point the balloon opening toward the starting support.
  3. Pull the straw to the starting mark, release the neck and observe the direction of motion.
  4. Repeat at least three times with similar inflation; then try a smaller or larger inflation and record each travel time.

Measure and explain

Measure distance s between two marks, and time t between them. Estimate average speed = s/t. For example, 2 m in 1.6 s gives 1.25 m/s. That is an average speed, not the balloon’s acceleration or thrust. What changes as the balloon empties? Where do friction and string sag affect the result?

Experiment further

Compare a taut string with a slightly sagging one, or a large balloon with a smaller balloon. Keep the string length and measurement method fixed. Sketch the forces acting on the balloon and describe why real rockets need guidance and stability control.
