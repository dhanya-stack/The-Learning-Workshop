Fluid Flow Experiments

Measure water flow and explore why pipe size and height matter.

The idea

Fluid flow links an everyday observation—water running through a tube—to important principles of conservation and pressure loss. With a timed collection experiment, we can estimate how much water passes a point and compare arrangements under similar conditions.

Read the step-by-step video text
  1. Prepare a stable setup: Arrange a water container, pre-filled tube and measuring cup.
  2. Use gravity: Keep the outlet lower than the water level and secure tubing.
  3. Collect the flow: Time how long the collected water takes to reach a mark.
  4. Calculate Q: Example: 250 mL in 20 s is a flow rate of 12.5 mL/s.
  5. Change one factor: Vary the tube length or diameter while keeping other conditions similar.
  6. Discuss the limits: Changing the water height changes the pressure head during a trial.

What you need

  1. Water — Use a small quantity.
  2. One stable elevated container — To provide a height difference.
  3. One short transparent flexible tube — Sized to fit the container safely.
  4. Tape — For securing the setup if needed.
  5. One measuring cup — To collect and measure water.
  6. One stopwatch or timer — To measure collection time.
  7. One towel — To catch spills.

Safety: Keep electronics away from water. Never prime the tube by mouth; avoid slippery floors and secure the container.

Build it

  1. Place a stable water container above a collecting cup. Fill the tubing with water before use and keep the tube outlet lower than the source water level.
  2. Secure the tubing and allow gravity to drive flow. Collect water for a measured 15–30 seconds.
  3. Measure the collected volume. Repeat three times, aiming for similar starting water levels.
  4. Repeat with a different tube length or diameter, changing only one factor at a time.

Measure and explain

Volumetric flow rate Q = V/t. Suppose 250 mL is collected in 20 s: Q = 12.5 mL/s = 1.25 × 10−5 m³/s. In a fully filled tube of internal diameter 6 mm, area A = π(0.003 m)² ≈ 2.83 × 10−5 m². Its mean speed is approximately v = Q/A ≈ 0.44 m/s. Note that changing water height during the test changes the pressure head; experiments are not perfectly steady.

Experiment further

Graph measured Q against tube length or starting water height. If the results scatter, check leaks, bubbles, tube kinks, and changing head. Why can a narrower tube reduce total flow while water within a constriction can move faster under different controlled conditions?
